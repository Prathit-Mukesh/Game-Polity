"Parliament is not a rubber stamp for the executive; it is a mirror of the people's will, and the Member of Parliament is its most vital
reflecting surface."
Constitutional and Functional Role of the Parliamentarian
1. Legislative Function
Introducing, debating, and voting on Bills (Government and Private
Member's Bills).
Scrutinising delegated legislation and subordinate rules.
Participating in Select/Joint Committees for clause-by-clause
examination of Bills.
2. Representative Function
Articulating constituency interests, regional aspirations, and social
group concerns (linguistic, religious, caste-based) in the national
forum.
Acting as a link between citizens and the state apparatus —
forwarding grievances, facilitating access to schemes.
3. Deliberative Function
Debating national policy, budgets, and matters of public importance
(via Zero Hour, Short Duration Discussions, Calling Attention
Motions).
Providing a "safety valve" for public opinion and dissent through
constitutionally sanctioned procedures.
4. Oversight/Accountability Function
Holding the executive accountable via Question Hour, No-
Confidence Motions, Adjournment Motions, and Committee scrutiny
(DRSCs, PAC, Estimates Committee, PUC).
Scrutinising CAG reports through the Public Accounts Committee.
5. Financial Control (Power of the Purse)
Approving the Union Budget, Demands for Grants, and Money Bills.
Historically the House of Commons' power over taxation ("no
taxation without representation") is the ancestor of this function.
6. Constituency Development Function
Utilising the MP Local Area Development Scheme (MPLADS) — ₹5
crore per annum per MP — for local infrastructure.
Grievance redressal through correspondence with ministries, use of
privilege to raise local issues.
7. Quasi-Judicial/Disciplinary Role (Collective)
Participating in impeachment of judges/President, disqualification
proceedings under the Tenth Schedule (via voting/party position),
and privilege matters.

Issues in Fulfilling the Role
A. Declining Legislative Productivity and Scrutiny
Falling sitting days: Lok Sabha sat for an average of ~127 days/year in the 1950s; this has fallen to under 60-70
days in recent Lok Sabhas.
Low referral to Committees: Only around 16% of Bills were referred to Departmentally Related Standing
Committees (DRSCs) in the 17th Lok Sabha, compared to over 60% in the 14th and 15th Lok Sabhas — meaning
most laws now bypass structured expert scrutiny.
Rushed passage: Several major legislations (e.g., the three erstwhile farm laws of 2020, later repealed) were passed
with minimal debate and without committee reference, triggering questions on deliberative quality.
B. Disruptions and Loss of Productive Time
Disruptions have become a routine tool of opposition protest rather than an exception. In several recent sessions,
Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha have functioned at less than 50% of scheduled time due to logjams over issues ranging
from price rise to allegations of financial impropriety.
In 2016, the Rajya Sabha was unable to take up Finance and Appropriation Bills for extended periods due to
protests, delaying critical financial legislation.
Adjournments sine die and repeated washouts of Question Hour erode the very oversight mechanisms MPs are
meant to use.
C. Anti-Defection Law and the "Whip" System
The Tenth Schedule (52nd Amendment, 1985) compels MPs to vote per party direction on pain of disqualification,
converting individual legislators into instruments of party command rather than independent deliberators.
This undermines:
Executive accountability: Ruling party MPs cannot meaningfully cross-vote even to hold their own government
accountable.
Parliament as deliberative body: Effective decision-making shifts from the full House to a handful of party
leaders.
Representative accountability to voters: MPs can deflect responsibility for unpopular votes onto the "party
whip."
Political engineering using defection remains rampant: the 2019 Karnataka crisis, and the 2022 Maharashtra
crisis , Tamil Nadu Assembly AIADMK Defections (2026), The Telangana BRS to Congress Defections (2024–
2025)
D. Weak Committee System
Average attendance in DRSC meetings has hovered around 49-50%, well below desirable levels.
DRSCs have only a one-year tenure, denying MPs the chance to build deep sectoral expertise.
Recommendations of committees like the PAC are not binding — even though ~80% are historically accepted by
government, non-binding status weakens teeth.
Inadequate research and technical staff support compared to legislatures like the US Congress.
E. Criminalisation of Politics and Declining Deliberative Quality
The Association for Democratic Reforms (ADR) has reported that a significant and rising share of sitting MPs face
criminal cases (including serious/heinous charges) — in the 2019 Lok Sabha, ADR found roughly 43% of MPs had
declared criminal cases, and around 29% had serious criminal cases pending.
This raises concerns about the quality of legislative deliberation and public trust in the institution.
F. Office of Profit and Conflict of Interest
Ambiguity in the definition of "office of profit" (Articles 102(1)(a), 191(1)(a)) has led to repeated controversies, e.g.,
disqualification of 20 Delhi MLAs in 2018 for holding Parliamentary Secretary posts, showing how conflicts
between legislative independence and executive proximity persist.
G. Constituency Work Overload vs. National Deliberation
MPs often prioritise MPLADS-driven constituency visibility and local grievance redressal over national policy
engagement, given electoral incentives reward visible local delivery over legislative scrutiny — a structural tension
between the representative and deliberative functions.
H. Parliamentary Privileges: Ambiguity and Misuse
Absence of codification of privileges creates uncertainty; the Supreme Court's ruling in Sita Soren v. Union of India
(2024) — overturning the 1998 P.V. Narasimha Rao judgment — held that legislators do not enjoy immunity from
prosecution for bribery in relation to a vote/speech, underscoring how privilege had previously been misused to
shield corrupt conduct.

Structural Reforms
Mandatory committee referral of all non-urgent Bills (as practiced in the UK), reversing the recent decline in referral rates.
Extend DRSC tenure beyond one year to build sectoral expertise and continuity (akin to US Congressional committees).
Codify and clarify parliamentary privileges to prevent misuse while protecting genuine legislative independence, recommended

Behavioural/Political Reforms
Differentiate the scope of whips — confine mandatory whips to confidence/money matters, allowing free votes on

Promote intra-party democracy so dissent within parties reduces the need for defection-driven crises (II ARC, 2007).
6Time-bound adjudication of anti-defection cases, per the Supreme Court's guidance in Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Speaker

Professionalise committee staff with domain experts, on the model of the US Congressional Research Service.
Introduce MP performance metrics — attendance, questions asked, private members' bills moved, committee participation —

Strengthen research support for individual MPs (akin to the UK's House of Commons Library) to improve the quality of floor

Electoral and Accountability-linked Reforms
Rebalance incentive structures so that legislative/deliberative performance (not just visible local spending via MPLADS) is
factored into public and party evaluation of MPs.
Fast-track adjudication of criminal cases against sitting legislators, as directed by the Supreme Court in various PILs, to address
the criminalisation of politics.
The parliamentarian is simultaneously a legislator, a representative, a watchdog, and a constituency servant — a role demanding both national vision and local responsiveness.

Impact
1.Legislators
a.In a parliamentary system, legislators are expected to exercise their independent judgement while
determining their position on an issue.
b.The choice of the member may be based on a combination of public interest, constituency interests, and
party affiliations.
c.This fundamental freedom of choice could be undermined if the member is mandated to vote along the party
line on every Bill or motion.
d.One of the key features of a parliamentary democracy is that the government is accountable for its decisions
to Parliament. This accountability is tested through questions posed to Ministers, discussions on various
government policies, and by debating national issues.
e.However, the anti-defection law deters a legislator from his duty to hold the government accountable, by
requiring him to follow the instruction of the party leadership on almost every decision.
f.May debate and dissent from his party position on an issue in Parliament, but will still be compelled to vote
as per the instruction of the party whip
g.By prohibiting dissent, the anti-defection undermines the system of executive accountability to the
legislature, and gives the executive control over Parliament on all votes.
2.Parliament/Legislatures
a.Leads to major decisions in the legislature being taken by a few party leaders and not by the larger body of
legislators.
b.In India, political parties frequently issue whips on matters which are subject to a vote in Parliament. This
implies that anyone who controls the party leadership can issue directions to all legislators.
c.This reduces Parliament from a deliberative body to one where party leaders are able to unilaterally decide
the vote on an issue, without consulting with members of their political party.
d.As a result, to win a motion in Parliament, the government is only required to consult with leaders of the
major political parties in the House
e.House. This number for consensus may further be reduced if a single party has majority in the House.
3.Voter
a.Breaks the chain of accountability between elected representatives and the voter
b.Every member is required to vote as per the direction issued by their party.
c.He can easily justify his voting decisions and absolve herself of this representational responsibility to her
voters by merely saying that the party whip compelled her to vote in a particular way.

43 Congress MLAs led by Chief Minister Pema Khandu defected to the People’s Party of Arunachal (PPA),
eventually joining the BJP.
2019: Karnataka Legislative Assembly Crisis
Several MLAs resigned, leading to a political crisis and government collapse.

During a vote of confidence for the state government, 25 rebel MLAs belonging to the AIADMK voted contrary to the

Examples
The anti-defection law was meant to protect the mandate, not imprison the conscience of representatives. Its reform lies in striking
a balance between party stability and democratic deliberation.

Should the Anti-Defection Law Apply to the Rajya Sabha (Upper House)
MCN 2.0
 	Magna Carta Mains 2026

courses.atishmathur.com 	tracker.atishmathur.com 	upscpath.com
Recent political developments (AAP) involving defections by Rajya Sabha MPs have revived debate on whether an Upper House—
designed to be a House of reflection rather than government formation—should be subject to the same anti-defection discipline as
the directly elected Lok Sabha
Arguments for continuing ADL in Rajya Sabha
1.Party mandate extends to both Houses
a.Voters elect political parties—not merely
individuals.
b.Rajya Sabha MPs are elected by elected MLAs on
party lines.
c.Therefore, changing party after election amounts to
betrayal of the mandate.
2.Legislative stability
a.Many crucial Bills require Rajya Sabha approval.
b.Without party discipline, governments may face
legislative paralysis.
3.Prevents horse-trading
a.Even if governments do not fall, important
constitutional amendments, ordinary Bills,
impeachment motions, and parliamentary
committees, could become vulnerable to
inducements.
4.Preserves responsible party government
a.Cabinet Government
b.Collective Responsibility
c.Party Manifesto Governance
i.This requires legislative cohesion across both
Houses.
5.Prevents misuse of nominated seats
a.The Constitution specifically provides that
nominated members who join a party after six
months incur disqualification, indicating that
Parliament intended the anti-defection regime to
extend to Rajya Sabha as well.
Arguments against applying ADL to Rajya Sabha
1. Rajya Sabha is a deliberative chamber
a.Unlike Lok Sabha, its primary function is scrutiny
revisionexpert debate, federal representation
b.A whip converts independent deliberation into
mechanical voting.
2.Government survival is not at stake
a.The original evil sought to be prevented - government
collapse— cannot occur in Rajya Sabha.
b.Therefore, strict anti-defection provisions may be
disproportionate.
3.Weakens federalism -
a.Rajya Sabha members represent States, not merely
political parties.
b.Rigid whips often compel MPs to vote with national
party positions even where State interests differ.
4.Defeats bicameralism
a.A second chamber exists to provide second thought,
reconsideration, correction of legislative errors.
b.If members merely follow party instructions,
bicameralism loses much of its value.
5.Curtails freedom of speech
a.Articles 105 and parliamentary conventions envision
MPs as deliberative legislators.
b.Constant whip-driven voting reduces legislative
autonomy.
6.Weakens expertise
a.Rajya Sabha includes: economists, scientists, jurists,
artists, nominated members
b.Their value lies precisely in offering independent
judgment.
Anti-defection does NOT apply during Rajya Sabha elections conducted by MLAs because those elections are not proceedings of the
Legislative Assembly; parties may take disciplinary action, but voting contrary to party preference in such elections does not attract
disqualification under the Tenth Schedule. However, once elected, Rajya Sabha MPs are fully governed by the Tenth Schedule.
Balanced Way Forward : Rather than abolishing the Anti-Defection Law in the Rajya Sabha, a calibrated approach would
better preserve both stability and deliberation :
Retain disqualification for voluntarily giving up party membership, as this directly addresses political defections.
Restrict binding whips to confidence-related matters (where applicable), Money Bills, Constitutional Amendments
involving core party commitments, or other clearly specified categories.
Permit free voting on ordinary legislation, allowing the Rajya Sabha to function as a genuine chamber of review.
Impose a statutory time limit for the Chairman/Speaker to decide disqualification petitions, improving certainty and
preventing strategic delays.
Strengthen inner-party democracy, so dissent is accommodated within parties rather than suppressed through rigid
legislative control.
A Parliament that only counts numbers cannot become a House of wisdom. Constitutional morality demands that
while political stability is protected, legislative conscience must not be extinguished.

Intro
Parliamentary privileges are those which are enjoyed by the house collectively, its committees and its
members individually without which they cannot discharge their functions efficiently and effectively
In Sita Soren v. UoI (2024), 7-judge SC Bench while overruling P. V. Narasimha Rao v State (1998)
unanimously held that legislators are not protected with immunity under Article 105(2) and Article 194(2)
does not include immunity from bribery.
Rationale
To protect lawmakers from interference by others in the discharge of their
essential functions.
Differential treatment accorded to incumbent legislators is justified in light
of their duties as a particular class of public officials
The ability to speak and vote inside the legislature without the fear of
intimidation or punishment is considered to be intrinsic to the role of an
elected representative.
A breach of privilege is also an act of contempt of the House. Reverse not
true.
Judgements
1.M.S.M. Sharma vs. Shri Krishna Sinha (1959) (searchlight case)
a.Article 194(3) > Article 19(1)(a), since the former was a
special protection accorded to legislatures in contrast to the
general applicability of Art 19.
2.Dr Jatin Chandra Ghosh v Hari Sadhan (1961)
a.Acting in a private capacity does not invoke Article 194
3.Keshav Singh Case (1964) :
a.Legislative privileges are not absolute and are subject to
judicial review. The legislature cannot use its privileges to
violate fundamental rights.
4.Raja Ram Pal v Speaker, Lok Sabha (2007)
a.Punishment does not include future disqualifications.
5.Sita Soren Case (2024) : - two-fold test - Bribery not protected
a.The act for which immunity is claimed must be related to
the collective functions of the House.
b.Secondly, the immunity sought should have a “functional
relationship” to the discharge of the duty of an individual
legislator.
Issues
1.Codification
2.Conflict with Freedom of Speech & other FRs
3.Limits Judicial Review
4.Parliamentary Overreach
5.Politicisation
6.Excessive Usage
7.Separation of Powers
Solutions
1.Parliamentary Privileges Commission
2.Codification of Privileges
3.Training and Sensitisation of Legislators
4.Involvement of the Judiciary
5.Strengthening Parliamentary Committees
6.Promoting Cross-Party Dialogue and Consensus
7.Respect for Rights and Parliamentary Procedures
8.Rule 380 permits the Speaker to expunge words if
they are unparliamentary, defamatory, indecent, or
undignified. But it permits the Speaker to expunge
only the offending word not the sentences or the
paragraphs.
Parliamentary privileges are not ornamental relics of a bygone constitutional era, they are not individual entitlements but
institutional guarantees, designed to uphold the dignity and independence of the legislative body as a whole. In a
constitutional democracy governed by the rule of law, parliamentary privileges must serve democracy, not dominate it.

Intro The concept of Office of Profit lies at the confluence of constitutional morality, political ethics, and the
doctrine of separation of powers
Rationale
1.separation of power between the legislature and the
executive
2.prevention of possibility of a conflict between duty and
interest of an individual who is required to perform the
role of both a legislator and a member of the executive.
Developments
1.In 2018, on ECI recommendation, President disqualified
20 MLAs of the Delhi Legislative Assembly last month
for holding an ‘office of profit’ in form of Parliamentary
Secretaries to to various ministries in the Delhi
government.
2.Reports of parliamentary secretaries being appointed in
20 states in the past with court judgments striking down
these appointments in several cases
The law does not clearly define what constitutes an office of profit
The definition has evolved over the years with interpretations made in various
court judgments.
Three Essential Elements : S. Umrao Singh v. Darbara Singh (1968)
the individual holds an office
it is an office of profit
it is under the Government of India or a State
Nature of Control : Ashok Kumar Bhattacharya v. Ajoy Biswas (1985) ; Dobrey v.
Dobrey (1984)
The extent of government control over the office plays a pivotal role
Pecuniary Advantage : Hedge v. State of Karnataka (1993)
While an element of profit is necessary, mere prestige or advantages are
insufficient.
Regularity of Income : Hotilal v. Bahadur (1958)
An office need not have a regular income
Sui Juris: Ghulam Chand v. Thakur Singh (1953)
The holder of the office must be sui juris, meaning legally capable of holding the
office
Tests : Swapan Roy v. Pradyut Bordoloi (2001)
Government Appointment: Guru Govinda Basu v. Shankari; Prasad Ghosal &
Ors (1964)
The government must have the power to appoint and remove the holder of
the office
Government Payment:
The government should be responsible for paying remuneration attached to
the office
Government Control:
The government should exercise control over the performance of the
functions associated with the office.
Conflict of Interest:
The presence of a conflict of interest is a crucial factor in determining
whether an office falls within the ambit of an "Office of Profit."
Essentials
Second ARC Recommendations :
Offices in purely advisory bodies should
not be treated as offices of profit,
irrespective of any remuneration attached
with the office
Offices whose holder has executive
decision-making power and control over
public funds should be treated as offices
of profit. The executive decision-making
power may include deciding policy,
managing or approving expenditure etc.
If the office-holder is the Head or
Member of such organisation where close
coordination between the Council of
Ministers and the organisation is essential
for functioning of the Government, the
office should not be treated as an office
of profit.
Way forward
“A practical view, not pedantic basket of
tests, should guide in arriving at a
sensible conclusion “ Prof. Achary,
Practice and Procedure of Parliament

Issues
A. High threshold protects stability but makes accountability difficult
The requirement of a majority of all the then members prevents frivolous removal. But in a majoritarian House, it also means that a Speaker
backed by the ruling majority is almost impossible to remove, even if the Opposition’s allegations relate to procedural fairness or denial of
speaking opportunities. Thus, the process is formally available but politically very difficult.
B. The Speaker is judged by the same political House whose proceedings he controls
The Speaker is expected to be impartial, but the removal mechanism is political: MPs vote broadly on party lines. This makes the process
less like institutional accountability and more like a test of ruling-party numbers.
C. Admissibility rules can become gatekeeping tools
Rule 200A: the resolution must be specific, clearly expressed, not contain arguments, inferences, imputations, ironic expressions or
defamatory statements. This is sensible because allegations against the Speaker must not be vague or reckless. But it can also become a
procedural filter to reject motions on drafting defects.
D. The “50 members rising” requirement screens frivolity but weakens small Opposition groups
The requirement that at least 50 members rise ensures that the motion has minimum seriousness. But it also means that smaller
Opposition parties, even if raising a genuine institutional grievance, cannot trigger debate unless they secure broader coalition support.
E. No independent fact-finding mechanism
There is no preliminary independent committee to examine whether the Speaker violated rules, acted with bias, or denied fair participation.
The matter goes directly into a political debate and vote. In cases involving complex procedural allegations, the House may vote before
facts are objectively clarified.
F. Conflict with Speaker’s anti-defection role
The Speaker is also the adjudicating authority under the Tenth Schedule. This creates a major institutional tension: if a Speaker faces a
removal notice, should he continue deciding disqualification petitions that may affect the majority supporting or opposing him?
In Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker, the Supreme Court held that a Speaker facing a notice for removal should not decide disqualification
petitions. The judgment arose from the Arunachal Pradesh political crisis and is central to the debate on Speaker neutrality.
However, later cases and political crises show the difficulty of this rule: a mere notice for removal can itself be used tactically to paralyse
anti-defection proceedings.
G. Judicial review is limited
Article 122 protects parliamentary proceedings from being questioned in court merely on procedural irregularity. However, the Supreme
Court in Raja Ram Pal v. Speaker, Lok Sabha held that legislative proceedings may still be reviewed if there is substantive illegality,
unconstitutionality, mala fides, or violation of natural justice. Kaul and Shakdher also note this principle while discussing judicial review of
legislative privilege proceedings.
Thus, courts can intervene in extreme cases, but they generally avoid entering into the House’s internal procedure.
H. Absence of Deputy Speaker weakens neutral chairing
The Deputy Speaker is constitutionally envisaged as an independent office, not subordinate to the Speaker. . However, the Seventeenth
Lok Sabha had no Deputy Speaker, which exposes a practical gap: if a removal motion is moved, the House may depend on a member of
the Panel of Chairpersons rather than a constitutionally elected Deputy Speaker.

Solutions
A. Make the Deputy Speaker’s election time-bound
A constitutional convention or rule should require election of the Deputy Speaker within a fixed time, say 30 or 60
days from the first sitting. Since the Deputy Speaker may preside in sensitive situations involving the Speaker, leaving
the post vacant weakens institutional balance.
B. Create a preliminary scrutiny committee
Before debate, a small all-party committee or rules committee could examine whether the notice is specific, non-
defamatory, and based on identifiable procedural acts. This would prevent frivolous motions while reducing the
perception that admissibility is controlled by the very institutional structure being challenged.
C. Clarify Rule 200A through objective drafting standards
The rules should prescribe a standard format
D. Provide guaranteed debate time after threshold support
Once 50 members support the motion and the notice period is complete, the matter should be mandatorily listed
within a defined period. This would prevent the motion from being delayed or buried in the business agenda.
E. Require recorded voting
Given the constitutional significance of removing a Speaker, voting should normally be by division or recorded vote,
not merely voice vote. This would increase transparency and allow citizens to know how their representatives voted.
F. Separate Speaker’s adjudicatory role from political majority
The deeper reform is to reconsider the Speaker’s role under the Tenth Schedule. In Keisham Meghachandra Singh v.
Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly, the Supreme Court stated that the Speaker acting under the Tenth Schedule
must decide disqualification petitions within a reasonable period, normally around three months, and even suggested
that Parliament may consider an independent tribunal mechanism.
This reform would reduce the political stakes attached to the Speaker’s office and thereby reduce the incentive to use
removal motions tactically.
G. Adopt a stronger convention of Speaker neutrality
India may evolve a convention that once elected, the Speaker distances himself from active party politics. The
Speaker’s authority depends less on removal rules and more on the perceived impartiality of rulings, allocation of
speaking time, recognition of Opposition, certification of Money Bills, anti-defection decisions, and disciplinary
powers.
The removal process of the Speaker reflects a careful constitutional balance: the Speaker must be protected from casual political
attacks, but not insulated from accountability

The Vice-President occupies India’s second-highest
constitutional office, but the office is unusual: its normal
function is legislative—as the ex officio Chairman of the Rajya
Sabha—while its contingent function is executive—as the
constitutional substitute for the President. It is therefore best
understood as a constitutional hinge connecting parliamentary
functioning with executive continuity.
Strengths
1. Constitutional continuity
The Vice-President ensures that the Presidency never remains
functionally vacant.
2. Stable leadership of Rajya Sabha
Since the Vice-President is elected for five years and is not a Rajya
Sabha member retiring every six years, the office provides continuity
to a periodically changing House.
3. Institutional distance from constituency politics
The Vice-President does not represent a constituency and does not
depend on re-election to Parliament, which can facilitate impartial
decision-making.
4. Bicameral legitimacy
Election by both Houses gives the Chairman wider parliamentary
legitimacy than election by the Rajya Sabha alone.
Weaknesses and tensions
1. Partisan election versus impartial office
Candidates are usually nominated by political alliances. Once elected,
however, the Vice-President is expected to function above party. The
Constitution provides no formal “de-partisanisation” mechanism
comparable to conventions surrounding some presiding officers in
other parliamentary systems.
2. Lok Sabha predominance
Although the Vice-President chairs the Rajya Sabha, Lok Sabha
members form the larger part of his electoral college and can
decisively influence the result.
3. Limited federal character
State Legislatures do not participate even though the Vice-President
presides over the Council of States.
4. Vague removal standard
The absence of specified grounds provides flexibility but produces
uncertainty and scope for partisan conflict.
5. Broad procedural discretion
Decisions over admissibility, suspension, expunction, privilege and
anti-defection have significant political consequences. Exercise of
such powers without adequately reasoned orders can create
perceptions of arbitrariness.
6. No fixed deadline for casual vacancy
Article 68 requires an election “as soon as possible” but sets no outer
limit, unlike the six-month requirement applicable to a Presidential
vacancy.
Way forward
A. Strengthen conventions of impartiality
Upon election, the Vice-President should visibly distance himself
from active party organisation and ensure balanced opportunities
for the Government, Opposition and smaller parties.
B. Reasoned procedural rulings
Important rulings involving suspension, admissibility, privilege,
anti-defection or removal notices should ordinarily contain brief,
publicly accessible reasons. Transparency strengthens rather than
weakens the authority of the Chair.
C. Codify removal-notice procedure
Rajya Sabha rules or directions could clarify:
the authority before whom notice is filed;
essential contents of the proposed resolution;
preliminary scrutiny standards;
timelines and opportunity to correct defects;
the role of the Deputy Chairman where the notice concerns
the Chairman.
Such rules must facilitate accountability without allowing frivolous
motions.
D. Time-bound quasi-judicial decisions
Resignation and defection cases should be decided within a
reasonable, defined period, subject to exceptional circumstances.
E. Encourage consensus candidatures
Greater consultation between the Government and Opposition
can enhance the moral authority of the Vice-President, especially
because the office requires cooperation across party lines.
F. Clarify the vacancy timeline
Parliament may consider prescribing an indicative statutory
timeframe for conducting an election after a casual vacancy, while
retaining sufficient flexibility for extraordinary circumstances.
The office combines three constitutional values:
continuity of the State, neutrality of the Chair and dignity of
parliamentary deliberation.
Its authority rests less on extensive independent executive power and
more on credibility, restraint and procedural fairness

2. Open ballot creates a transparency–autonomy dilemma
Open ballot has two competing consequences:
Advantages
makes covert cross-voting more difficult;
enables parties to detect betrayal;
reduces anonymity behind which inducements may operate;
reflects the party-based character of proportional representation.
Does not prevent bribery where the party itself, its agent or a rival political formation participates in the transaction.
Thus, transparency towards the party does not necessarily produce transparency towards the public.
3. Uncertain relationship with the anti-defection law
Paragraph 2(1)(b) of the Tenth Schedule deals with voting or abstention contrary to a party direction in the House. A Rajya
Sabha election is administered by the Election Commission and is not an ordinary legislative vote on the floor of the
Assembly.
Consequently, a contrary vote in a Rajya Sabha election does not automatically result in disqualification under the voting-
against-direction limb of the Tenth Schedule. A party may take disciplinary action, expel the MLA or rely on broader conduct
to argue that the member has “voluntarily given up” party membership, but the result is uncertain and depends on facts and
adjudication.
This creates a gap: The vote is open enough for the party to identify dissent, but not necessarily covered by an automatic
constitutional penalty. Making every cross-vote a ground for automatic disqualification, however, would transform MLAs
entirely into delegates of party leadership and remove the possibility of legitimate dissent.
4. Money power and bribery
Rajya Sabha elections involve a small and identifiable electoral college. Where only a few additional votes are required,
individual MLAs acquire disproportionate bargaining power.
The problem is aggravated by: weak transparency concerning negotiations between candidates and legislators;
movement of MLAs to hotels or resorts; strategic abstentions and invalid ballots; delayed criminal investigation;
election petitions that may remain pending for a substantial part of the member’s term. The 2024 Sita Soren ruling closes an
important legal loophole by holding that legislative privilege cannot shield bribery, but successful reform depends on prompt
investigation, evidence gathering and trial.
5. Party high-command control over nominations
Although MLAs formally elect members, candidates are normally selected by national or State party leadership.
This can result in:
limited consultation with party legislators;
nomination of financiers, celebrities or politically convenient leaders;
denial of opportunity to grassroots workers;
trading of Rajya Sabha nominations as instruments of coalition management;
legislators being presented with a predetermined party slate rather than a genuine choice.
Therefore, the proportionality of the election does not necessarily ensure internal democracy in candidate selection.

8. Frequent unopposed elections
Where parties nominate only as many candidates as their Assembly arithmetic permits, candidates are returned
unopposed.
Unopposed returns are legally efficient and avoid a meaningless poll, but they may:
turn the election into an allocation made by party leadership;
reduce legislative scrutiny of candidates;
deprive smaller parties and independents of an opportunity to contest;
reinforce the perception that Rajya Sabha seats are negotiated rather than electorally earned.
9. Complexity and invalid ballots
STV counting is more complex than ordinary first-past-the-post voting. Outcomes may turn on:
incorrect preference marking;
duplicate preference numbers;
use of an unauthorised mark or instrument;
refusal to display the ballot;
transfer values and elimination rounds;
disputed rejection of a small number of ballots.
Because margins are often narrow, one invalid ballot may alter the result. The Haryana-related objections during the 2026
election cycle illustrate the continuing significance of ballot validity disputes.
10. Opacity in presidential nominations
The Constitution identifies broad fields for the 12 nominated members but does not prescribe:
a transparent search process;
public invitations for nominations;
objective selection criteria;
consultation with an independent body;
a requirement to publish detailed reasons
Improvements
A. Preserve the core system
The PR-STV system should be retained because it:
gives representation to more than the largest party;
reflects the relative strength of parties in the Assembly;
prevents a bare majority from automatically capturing all seats;
accommodates second and subsequent preferences;
supports political plurality.
Replacing it with first-past-the-post would allow the ruling party in a State to monopolise Rajya Sabha representation.
B. Retain a restricted open ballot, not a fully public vote
A return to complete secrecy may revive anonymous cross-voting and inducements. Conversely, publication of every MLA’s ballot
would permit intimidation and excessive party control.
The preferable balance is:
disclosure only to the authorised party agent;
prohibition on photography, copying or wider circulation of ballots;
independent ECI observers at every polling station;
written recording of disputes involving refusal to show or rejection of ballots;
immediate videographic preservation of the relevant procedural event without recording the actual ballot choice;
expedited judicial review of ballot-rejection disputes.

C. Democratise party nominations
Political parties should be required to disclose:
the process through which the candidate was selected;
whether the State unit and party legislators were consulted;
the candidate’s qualifications and contribution to public life;
the candidate’s relationship with the State;
reasons for preferring the candidate over local aspirants.
Possible models include:
internal election by party MLAs;
an elected party collegium;
a shortlist prepared by the State unit;
recorded consultation between national and State leadership.
This would address the root problem rather than merely punishing MLAs after cross-voting.
D. Introduce a “State representation statement”
Restoring a rigid domicile requirement may lead to artificial residence certificates and exclude nationally distinguished
candidates.
A better middle path would require every candidate to publish a State Representation Statement explaining:
previous residence or work in the State;
public service connected with the State;
knowledge of its economic, cultural and federal concerns;
issues the candidate intends to raise in Parliament;
consultations undertaken with State stakeholders.
The requirement would create political accountability without imposing a narrow legal domicile barrier.
E. Make presidential nominations more transparent
For the 12 nominated seats, an advisory mechanism could include:
a non-partisan search committee;
nominations from national academies, universities and recognised institutions;
objective criteria of eminence and public contribution;
attention to regional, gender and disciplinary diversity;
publication of a brief statement explaining the nominee’s qualifications.
The President’s formal constitutional power may be retained while improving the credibility of the underlying selection
process.
F. Long-term federal reform
A future constitutional review may examine degressive proportionality:
every State receives a guaranteed minimum;
additional seats are allocated according to population;
the rate of increase slows for the largest States.
This could strengthen smaller States without importing the complete equality of the US Senate, under which vastly unequal
populations possess identical representation.
G. Reform unopposed elections
Unopposed candidates need not face a symbolic poll, but democratic scrutiny should continue through:
timely publication of nominations and affidavits;
a statutory period for public objections relating to eligibility and false declarations;
an interaction with all parties represented in the Assembly;
a public statement of legislative priorities;
publication of the party’s reasons for nomination.

Intro
The term "whip" originates from England's hunting fields, where a "whipper-in" kept stray hounds
within the pack. Politically, it was first used by Edmund Burke to describe rallying party supporters
‘Important for parliamentary democracy as parties see voting on some matters as how their politics is
perceived’ - Former Sec Gen ADT Achary
Anti-Defection Law (1985) legally enforces compliance with
whips to maintain party coherence and avoid opportunistic
switching of sides
Minister of Parliamentary Affairs and MoS Parliamentary
Affairs are Govt Chief Whips in LS and RS
Opposition Parties have their own whips
Status in India
Role
1.Ensuring Party Discipline: Whips direct party members to vote in line with the party’s stance, maintaining unity and
coherence in parliamentary decisions
2.Ensuring Attendance: Whips ensure members are present during important votes to maintain quorum and
demonstrate party strength.
3.Counting Votes: Before votes, whips survey members to predict outcomes and strategise accordingly.
4.Communication Link: Whips act as liaisons between party leadership and members, conveying party policies and
gathering feedback.
5.Managing Parliamentary Business: Whips coordinate which members speak on bills and manage internal party
organisation within the legislature.
6.Monitoring Discontent: Whips identify signs of dissent or dissatisfaction among members and report to party leaders
to preempt rebellion.
7.Enforcing Consequences: Violation of whip directives, especially a strict "three-line whip," can lead to disciplinary
action including disqualification under anti-defection laws or withdrawal of party membership
8.Crisis Management: During internal dissent, floor-crossing threats, or coalition instability, whips act as political
stabilisers.
Issues
1.Restriction on MPs' Freedom
2.Undemocratic Nature
3.Abuse of Power
4.Factionalism and Internal Party
Divides
5.Lack of Transparency.
6.Over-centralisation:
Best Practices
1.UK - breaking 3 line whip
makes MP independent
2.US - less formal, mostly for
legislative strategy
3.Australia - "Conscience votes"
are officially recognised
Solutions
1.Codify the Scope of Whips
a.170th Law Comm (1999)
2.Differentiate Legislative Issues
a.NCRWC (2002)
3.Internal Democracy in Parties
a.II ARC (2007)
4.Whip Transparency
a.PRS (2022)
5.Revise Anti-Defection Law
a.Former VP - H. Ansari
As India completes over 75 years of parliamentary democracy, perhaps it is time to reimagine the role of the Whip—
not as a symbol of control, but as a tool of coordination; not as an enforcer of silence, but as an enabler of responsible
freedom. For when discipline aligns with dialogue, and loyalty walks hand in hand with liberty, democracy does not
fracture—it flourishes.

Specific Case Studies of Parliamentary Committees
1.Standing Committee on Transport’s scrutiny of the Motor Vehicles
(Amendment) Bill, 2016 led to key reforms like removing the cap on
third-party insurance liability
2.Committee on External Affairs flagged the lack of preparedness at
Doklam and recommended border infrastructure strengthening
3.On average, the PAC has made 180 recommendations every year in the
past eight years, out of which 80% were accepted by the government.
4.PAC exposed irregularities in CWG 2010 and Defence land misuse,
recovering ₹838 crore in lease losses
5.EC flagged over-dependence on imported uranium, recommending
increased domestic mining and financial allocation
6.Standing Committee on Transport, Tourism, and Culture examined the
Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Bill, 2016, leading to significant
improvements in the legislation.
7.The Standing Committee on Railways recommended waiving dividend
payments by Indian Railways in 2015 to improve its financial health,
which was implemented in 2016.
General Solutions
1.Institutional Reforms:
a.Make Referral of Bills Mandatory (as in the UK).
b.Codify Time Allotment for report reading before budget vote.
c.Create a Constitution Committee for reviewing CA compulsorily
2.Merge overlapping committees to reduce cost and redundancy (e.g., merge PAC, COPU with DRSCs)
3.Functional Reforms:
a.Train MPs in committee functioning, policy evaluation, and budget literacy
b.Professionalise Committee Staff with domain expertise.
c.Introduce Performance Metrics (e.g., Reports per sitting ratio, quality of recommendations, etc.).
4.Procedural Reforms:
a.Ensure floor discussion of all major committee reports.
b.Create a Committee Calendar to align sittings with Parliament schedule (3-day Parliament, 2-day Committee model)
5.Technological Reforms:
a.Develop a real-time information system, integrated with government dashboards and CAG reports.
b.Allow for live citizen feedback on pending Bills via digital platforms.
OECD reports that countries with strong committee systems have better governance indicators, lower budgetary leakages, and
more informed legislation. If India seeks
'Maximum Governance', it must also commit to Maximum Accountability', starting with an empowered and effective Parliament.
Reforming the committee system is not an option; it is a democratic imperative. For in the invisible rooms of committee
deliberation lies the real defence of our visible democracy.
