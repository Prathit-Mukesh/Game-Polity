Essential Religious Practices (ERP) Doctrine
Meaning and Evolution
Originated in The Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments v. Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar of Shirur Mutt (1954).
Doctrine determines which religious practices are “essential and integral” to a religion and hence protected under Articles 25–26.
The Court, not the clergy, decides what constitutes an essential practice, using the test:
“Would the absence of the practice fundamentally alter the religion?” (Acharya Jagadishwarananda Avadhuta, 2004).
This judicial approach attempts to balance religious freedom with constitutional morality, equality, and social reform.
Important Cases Relating to ERP
Petitions challenging Sabrimala Judgement via a review petition, inheritance rights under Shariat Law, -
present a larger constitutional opportunity to freshly understand the extent of State intervention in
Religion as well as protection to certain religious activities.

Critical Analysis of the Essential Religious Practices (ERP) Doctrine
1.Judicial Overreach and Theological Adjudication
a.Transfers theological authority from religious communities to the judiciary.
i.In Shirur Mutt (1954), the Court held that “what constitutes the essential part of a religion is to be ascertained with
reference to the doctrines of that religion itself.” However, in later cases such as Durgah Committee v. Syed Hussain
Ali (1961) and Seshammal v. State of Tamil Nadu (1972), the Court began determining for itself what constituted
essential practices
b.This judicial role in defining theology has been criticised as antithetical to secularism, which requires the State (and by
extension, courts) to maintain neutrality towards religion rather than interpret it.
2.Inconsistency and Subjectivity in Application
a.Lack of clear parameters—the Court’s reasoning has oscillated between theological, moral, and reformist approaches.
i.In Syedna Taher Saifuddin (1962), excommunication was held an essential practice protected under Articles 25–26
ii.In Sabarimala (2018), the exclusion of women was ruled not an essential religious practice; while in Jallikattu (2023),
animal sport was upheld as a cultural right under Article 29 rather than religion.
3. Tension Between ERP and Constitutional Morality
a.Clashes with the principle of constitutional morality and individual rights:
i.Sabarimala (2018) and Shayara Bano (2017) signify a shift toward prioritising gender equality and dignity over
religious claims.
b.Justice D.Y. Chandrachud in Sabarimala asserted that constitutional morality, not religious orthodoxy, must guide judicial
reasoning.
i. This reflects a movement from group rights to individual rights, challenging the collective-centric foundation of ERP.
4. Ambiguity Between “Essential” and “Peripheral” Practices
a.The doctrine presumes a clear division between religious and secular, or essential and non-essential spheres. However, in
lived religion, such distinctions are often blurred:
b.Rituals, festivals, and customs are deeply embedded in cultural and social identity; classifying them as “non-essential”
disregards their sociological significance.
i.In Acharya Jagadishwarananda (2004), the Court denied protection to the Tandava dance, reasoning it was not
integral to Ananda Marga faith despite its scriptural sanction
5.ERP as a Tool for State Control
a.The doctrine has inadvertently legitimised State intervention under the guise of social reform:
i.Laws such as the Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments Acts have been upheld, allowing State supervision of
temple administration.
b.Scholars argue that this shifts the balance away from religious autonomy and leads to a form of “administrative
secularism”, wherein the State acts as arbiter of religious authenticity
6.The Constitutional Irony
a.While Articles 25–26 were intended to guarantee religious pluralism, the ERP doctrine’s selective protection undermines
that pluralism.
b.By declaring certain practices “inessential,” the Court effectively reforms religions from above, an exercise arguably
contrary to the secular spirit of the Constitution.
i.Akhil Bharatiya Soshit Karamchari Sangh v. Union of India (1981) observed that secularism implies equal respect for
all religions, not judicial censorship of their content.
Scholarly and Reform Proposals
1.Replace ERP with “Sincerity of Belief” Test:
a. Inspired by U.S. jurisprudence (e.g., Sherbert v. Verner, 1963), this test would protect any practice sincerely held as
religious, unless it violates public order, health, or morality.
2.Adopt a “Proportionality” Approach:
a. The Court should balance religious freedom against competing constitutional rights, as suggested in Bijoe Emmanuel v.
State of Kerala (1986).
3.Emphasise “Constitutional Morality Framework”:
a. Align religious freedom with the broader values of equality, liberty, and fraternity instead of relying on theological
validation

Critical Analysis of minority educational institutions
Strengths / Justifications
Protection of Cultural & Religious Identity
Article 30(1) empowers minorities to establish and administer educational institutions. This enables them to preserve language,
religion, culture, and ethos in a plural society.
Counter-majoritarian Safeguard
In a democracy where majority rules, the minority right to autonomy in education acts as a check against homogenising pressures
and majoritarian cultural domination.
Encouragement of Diversity and Experimentation
MEIs can adopt curricular or pedagogical innovations suited to their community’s needs, thereby enriching the broader educational
ecosystem.
Bridge for Marginalised Minorities
For socially or economically disadvantaged religious/linguistic minorities, MEIs can provide access to culturally sensitive education
where mainstream schools may be unwelcoming.
Critiques, Tensions, and Risks
Uneven Service to the Minority Community
Some institutions claim minority status but may not meaningfully serve the disadvantaged sections within that minority. A critical
commentary observes:
In practice, high-fee minority colleges may disproportionately benefit affluent members, undermining the solidaristic intention of
Article 30.
Exemption from Uniform Regulatory Standards
MEIs often claim immunity from certain regulatory norms (e.g., fee limits, teacher qualifications, reservation mandates). This can lead
to quality gaps, opacity, and misuse.
For instance, in Anjuman Ishaat E Taleem Trust v. State of Maharashtra (2025), SC held that the requirement to pass Teacher
Eligibility Test (TET) under the Right to Education Act applies to MEIs unless fully covered by Article 30 immunity.
SC has expressed concern that exempting minority schools from the RTE Act may weaken the universal education vision.
Fragmentation and Parallel Schooling
Carving out special exemptions for MEIs fragments the public schooling model and undermines integration. The Supreme Court
itself expressed that exemptions may reinforce divides rather than foster shared learning spaces.
Regulatory Capture and Malpractice
The autonomy accorded to MEIs can sometimes be misused to evade governance norms, leading to nepotism, corruption, opaque
admissions, or financial irregularities.
There have been scholarship scams tied to minority institutions, raising concerns over oversight.
Equity vs. Rights Conflict
The rights of individual students (within or outside the community) may conflict with the institution’s autonomy.
For instance, if an MEI discriminates (within its admission or staff policies) in a way that disadvantages some groups, how far
should courts allow it in the name of minority rights?
The tension between collective group rights (minority institutions managing themselves) and individual rights to equality is difficult
to balance.
Lack of Clear Criteria and Ambiguity
Determining which institutions legitimately deserve minority status has been contested, leading to legal disputes. The Supreme
Court’s 2024 ruling attempted to clarify the test for classifying minority institutions under equality guarantees. The lack of empirical
criteria or transparent audits means many claims rest on subjective assessments.
Unequal State Aid
While Article 30(2) prohibits discrimination in granting aid on the ground of minority management, in practice many MEIs (especially
unaided ones) receive little or no state support, placing them at a disadvantage relative to well-funded private institutions.
Capacity Constraints
Many MEIs—especially in rural or underprivileged regions—lack infrastructure, qualified teachers, learning materials, or funding. The
aspiration of autonomy clashes with ground realities.
Balancing Autonomy and Accountability
How much regulation is “reasonable”? Excessive state control undermines autonomy; too little oversight leads to poor quality or abuses.
Ensuring Inclusivity
MEIs may become insular or cater only to their community’s elites rather than being inclusive. A challenge is to ensure they remain
open, plural, and responsive to diversity within their community.
Legal Uncertainty & Litigation Burden
Because many rules about MEIs lie in judicial interpretation (e.g. scope of regulation, permissible limits), frequent litigation disrupts
functioning and adds cost.
Intersection with RTE and National Standards
With the Right to Education and other national benchmarks, the space for MEI autonomy is under pressure. The tension between
standardization and diversity is a continual battleground.
Uttarakhand Assembly passed the Uttarakhand State Authority for Minority Education (USAME) Bill, 2025 which replaces the Madrasa
Board and brings all minority educational institutions (Muslims, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis, Christians) under USAME.

Communalism
Religion as a Personal and Ethical Force
Represents private faith, devotion, and moral
guidance.
Inward-looking — promotes compassion through
acts like Langar (Sikhism), Zakat (Islam), Seva
(Hinduism).
Compatible with secularism: freedom to profess,
practice, and propagate (Articles 25–28).
Encourages unity and ethical conduct, aligning
with constitutional fraternity.
Communalism as Political Instrumentalisation of Religion
Defined as the political mobilisation of people based on
religious identity.
According to Rajni Kothari, it is “the instrumental use of religion
for political mobilisation.”
Bipan Chandra : it’s a modern phenomenon emerging during
colonial rule.
Key features:
Reduces religion to politics.
Promotes a zero-sum outlook: one group’s gain = another’s
loss.
Legitimises power politics through religion.
Erodes national integration and democratic discourse.
Roots of Communalism
Historical–Colonial Foundations
British ‘Divide and Rule’: Institutionalised communal distinctions.
Morley–Minto Reforms (1909): Introduced separate electorates.
Communal Award (1932): Religion-based reservations.
Census Classifications: Reinforced religious identity.
Partition Legacy (1947): Trauma and mistrust carried into post-independence politics.
Political and Institutional Drivers
Vote-bank politics: Religion-based mobilisation (Ram Janmabhoomi, Triple Talaq debates).
Weak enforcement: Hesitancy to act against hate speech (Tehseen Poonawalla vs Union of India, 2018).
Media sensationalism: Polarising coverage of riots.
Minority appeasement vs majoritarianism: Dual narratives deepen divides.
Socio-Economic Catalysts
Poverty, unemployment, and inequality → mass vulnerability to divisive propaganda.
Sachar Committee Report (2006): Muslims lag behind SCs/STs in education, credit access, and employment.
Relative deprivation: Community grievance transforms into communal identity (Pasmanda Muslim movement).
Livelihood conflicts: Mob lynching over cattle slaughter; closure of slaughterhouses; economic boycotts.
Social–Cultural Factors
Orthodox religious leadership reinforcing exclusivity.
Historical narratives manipulated (e.g., Aurangzeb vs Shivaji; temple–mosque disputes like Gyanvapi–Kashi
Vishwanath).
Textbook communalisation and selective history portrayal.
Stereotyping and ghettoisation: minorities labelled “outsiders.”
Institutional and Governance Gaps
Delayed justice (1984 anti-Sikh riots, 2002 Gujarat riots).
Perceived state bias: CAA–NRC seen as discriminatory; Shaheen Bagh protests.
Intervention in minority institutions (Waqf Act debates).
“Love Jihad” laws in UP, MP, Karnataka—curtailing personal freedoms.
International and Global Factors
Global Islamophobia: Reinforces domestic prejudice.
Cross-border terrorism: Creates suspicion (26/11 attacks).
Foreign funding: Religious NGOs receive transnational support influencing local politics.
Communalism in India is not a clash of religions but a politicisation of faith—where religion, once a
moral compass, becomes a weapon of power.

Consequences of Communalism
Human and Psychological Costs
Fear, trauma, and displacement (Delhi 2020, Muzaffarnagar 2013).
PTSD among riot survivors; intergenerational fear transmission (1984 anti-Sikh riots).
Economic and Developmental Losses
Property destruction, job loss, and halted local economies.
Hindrance to urban investment and tourism (e.g., Gujarat post-2002).
Political and Democratic Impact
Reduction of minorities to “vote banks.”
Issue-based politics replaced by identity-based narratives.
Weakens democratic pluralism and parliamentary debate quality.
National Security and International Image
Communalism provides fertile ground for radicalisation (ISIS recruitment).
Undermines India’s global image as a secular democracy (USCIRF, EU reports).
Creates policy paralysis — governments avoid reforms fearing backlash (UCC, Minority Rights).
Globalisation and the Transformation of Communal Identities
Diluting Effects
Urban cosmopolitanism (Bengaluru, Mumbai) fosters inter-religious coexistence.
Shared consumer culture (IPL, Bollywood, OTT) transcends divisions.
Global diaspora fosters inclusive celebration (Diwali in Times Square).
Youth individualism prioritises career and autonomy over religious loyalty.
Reinforcing Effects
Identity insecurity → revivalist communalism (“protecting cultural purity”).
Transnational religious networks (Ummah consciousness, global Hindutva).
Digital polarisation — social media amplifies hate and misinformation.
Global conflicts (Israel–Palestine, Taliban, ISIS) echoed domestically.
Net Effect
Dual dynamic: cosmopolitan tolerance in metros vs conservative revivalism in rural areas.
Class and generational divides — urban youth more global, rural poor more vulnerable to mobilisation.

Issues
1.Non-justiciable Nature: Fundamental duties are not legally enforceable. Their
non-justiciable character means citizens have no legal compulsion to follow
them, making them more of moral obligations.
2.Ambiguity and Vagueness: Many duties are criticized for being vague or broad
(e.g., "to strive towards excellence"), making them difficult to interpret and
implement in real-life scenarios.
3.Limited Scope: The duties do not cover certain essential civic behaviors, such
as paying taxes or voting, which were recommended by the Swaran Singh
Committee but were not included.
4.Overlap and Redundancy: Several duties overlap with existing laws or policies,
leading to redundancies and confusion.
5.Placement in the Constitution: They are placed after Directive Principles of
State Policy (Part IV), reducing their perceived significance when compared to
fundamental rights.
