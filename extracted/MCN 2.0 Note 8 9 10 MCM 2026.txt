Caste is a hereditary, endogamous, hierarchical, and occupational system of social stratification unique to the
Indian subcontinent.
Intro
From voting behaviour to access to opportunities, caste remains a central social institution shaping life chances.
Recent movements like Maratha and Jat reservations, Bihar Caste Survey (2023), and Rohini Commission (2024)
debates reaffirm its persistence.
Factors Reinforcing Caste in Contemporary India
Caste-based vote mobilisation:
Parties distribute tickets and portfolios using caste arithmetic.
Eg: Yadav–Muslim consolidation in UP (SP), Dalit-Bahujan base of BSP, Patidar mobilisation in Gujarat,
Rise of caste-based political parties:
RJD, DMK, JD(U), AIADMK, PMK, LJP, Apna Dal, Nishad Party — all draw strength from specific caste blocs.
Electoral reservation:
SC/ST seat reservations ensure representation but also reinforce caste identity as a political resource.
Economic and Occupational Continuity
Occupational persistence : Despite modernisation, lower castes remain concentrated in menial work — manual scavenging, sanitation,
informal labour. - Safai Karamchari Andolan (2023): ~85% manual scavengers are Dalits.
Caste in entrepreneurship:
Agarwal Vaishyas in trade, Nadars in business, Chettiars in finance, Reddys and Kammas in real estate.
Oxfam India 2022 found upper castes dominate high-paying private jobs; Dalits & Adivasis under-represented in formal sector.
Social and Cultural Reinforcement
Endogamy & marriage patterns:
IHDS-II: ~90% marriages remain intra-caste.
Matrimonial sites like Shaadi.com and Jeevansathi include caste filters; “Caste No Bar” matches <10%.
Religious and ritual continuity:
Temple priesthood, festivals, and purity rules still caste-based in many states.
Eg: Sabarimala controversy highlighted Brahminical priesthood norms.
Private sphere bias:
Housing segregation (Jain-only societies in Mumbai, Patidar colonies in Surat).
Dining taboos, domestic help discrimination persist.
Institutional and Educational Discrimination
Campus bias:
Darshan Solanki case (IIT Bombay, 2023) triggered UGC’s new anti-discrimination guidelines.
NLSIU Bangalore introduced a Diversity and Inclusion Cell post Dalit student complaints.
Rohini Commission (2023):
Found 97% OBC benefits captured by 25% sub-castes → intra-group inequality reinforcing caste segmentation.
Caste in Law, Data and Governance
Crime data: NCRB 2022: 57,582 crimes against SCs, 13,535 against STs — over 150 caste crimes daily.
Caste census politics:
Bihar Caste Survey 2023 reignited national debates on proportional representation and welfare targeting.
Affirmative action identity effects:
Reservation debates (EWS, OBC sub-categorisation) sustain caste salience in state policy.
Digital and Media Reinforcement
Caste-based social media groups:
YouTube channels and influencers promoting caste pride (#JaatPride, #DalitVoice, #MarathaUnity).
Popular culture representation:
Films (Article 15, Jai Bhim, Maharaj, Kathal) foreground caste injustice, increasing visibility but also identity assertion.

Articles 14–17: guarantee equality, prohibit untouchability, and permit affirmative action.

Indra Sawhney (1992) – introduced OBC “creamy layer” concept, balancing merit and equity.
Educational and Economic Mobility
Rising representation:
SC/ST/OBC representation growing in bureaucracy and higher education.
DoPT (2023): ~50% UPSC entrants from reserved categories.

Anonymity in metros:
Mixed neighbourhoods and shared workplaces reduce overt discrimination.

Inter-caste friendships:
College hostels, corporate settings, and cities encourage social mixing.

Cultural pluralism:

Globalisation and Diaspora Influence
Global exposure:

SC on Education Reservation
State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951)
Declared castebased reservations in educational institutions unconstitutional under Art 29(2),
leading to the First Constitutional Amendment (1951), which inserted Art 15(4).
Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992)
Upheld 27% OBC reservation in employment and introduced the concept of the "creamy
layer" for OBCs but ruled that reservations do not apply to promotions. This principle was
later extended to educational institutions.
T.M.A. Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka (2002)
Held that private unaided institutions have the right to admit students but cannot be forced
to implement state reservations.
P.A. Inamdar v. State of Maharashtra (2005)
Ruled that reservations cannot be imposed on private unaided institutions, leading to the
93rd Constitutional Amendment, inserting Art 15(5).
Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022)
Upheld the 103rd Constitutional Amendment, validating the 10% EWS reservation.
SC on Employment Reservation
Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992):
Upheld OBC reservations under Art 16(4) but excluded reservations in promotions.
Capped total reservations at 50%, barring exceptional circumstances.
Introduced the concept of the "creamy layer" to exclude economically advanced sections
within OBCs from reservation benefits.
M. Nagaraj v. Union of India (2006):
Upheld constitutional amendments allowing reservation in promotions (Arts 16(4A) and
16(4B)).
Mandated states to provide quantifiable data on backwardness, inadequate representation,
and administrative efficiency before implementing such reservations.
Rajeev Kumar Gupta v. Union of India (2016):
Allowed reservation in promotions for persons with disabilities under the Persons with
Disabilities Act, 1995, stating it does not violate Art 16.
Jarnail Singh v. Lachhmi Narain Gupta (2018):
Removed the requirement to prove "backwardness" for SCs/STs while implementing
promotion reservations.
Reaffirmed that states must collect quantifiable data on inadequate representation and
ensure administrative efficiency.
Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022):
Upheld the constitutionality of the 103rd Amendment, which introduced EWS reservations.
State of Punjab v Davinder Singh (2024)
Upheld the constitutional validity of subclassification in the Scheduled Caste and Scheduled
Tribe categories.

Constitutional basis (Art. 338): A constitutional watchdog to investigate/monitor SC safeguards, inquire into specific
complaints, advise on policy, and report to the President (whose ATRs must be laid before Parliament).
While inquiring/investigating it has civil-court powers (summon, documents, affidavits, commissions).
Governments must also consult it on major policy matters affecting SCs.
Composition/appointments: Chairperson, Vice-Chairperson and three Members, appointed by the President; it may regulate its
own procedure. (Tenure/conditions are set by Presidential rules/notifications.)

Receives complaints; calls for reports from authorities; conducts hearings/spot enquiries; issues recommendations to

reviews reservation & service safeguards; evaluates schemes;

~47,000 complaints over four years (2020-21 to 2024)

Karur, TN (Oct 2025): Visit following a fatal stampede; public assurance and recommendations (compassionate appointment

Powers
Civil-court-like powers are procedural, not punitive. Courts have repeatedly held NCSC’s reports/directions to be
recommendatory, not coercive:
SC, 1996 (All India Indian Overseas Bank SC/ST Employees’ Welfare Assn): Article 338(8) doesn’t give the Commission

Punjab & Haryana HC (Aug 2025): A State SC Commission cannot order FIR registration; it may recommend and flag lapses,
but not direct criminal process.

Section 69A — Blocking of Online Content
a.Provision : Empowers the Central Government (and authorised agencies) to direct any intermediary to block public
access to information on grounds of sovereignty/integrity of India, defence, security of the State, friendly relations
with foreign States, public order, or to prevent incitement to a cognisable offence.
b.Upheld in Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) — Supreme Court read down the provision, holding it
constitutionally valid only if procedural safeguards under the Blocking Rules, 2009 (reasoned order, opportunity of
hearing to originator, review committee) are strictly followed.
Key Issues
a.Lack of transparency: confidentiality clause (Rule 16, Blocking Rules 2009) keeps blocking orders/reasons secret,
hampering scrutiny and judicial review (e.g., 2022 Tanul Thakur case; X Corp's 2025 challenge to Sahyog portal
notices).
b.Absence of post-decisional hearing for content originators in most cases, violating natural justice
c.Overbroad, vague grounds ("public order", "sovereignty") permit subjective interpretation, risking misuse against
dissent and satire
d.No independent oversight — Review Committee is purely executive; no statutory judicial/parliamentary review
exists.
e.Chilling effect on speech (Art. 19(1)(a)): platforms tend to over-comply/over-block beyond legal requirement.
f.Emergency (no prior-hearing) blocking is used routinely rather than as exception, diluting Shreya Singhal safeguards

Section 79(3)(b) — Safe Harbour & Intermediary Liability
1.Provision : Section 79 grants intermediaries conditional immunity ("safe harbour") from liability for third-party content,
provided they act only as passive conduits and observe due diligence.
a.Section 79(3)(b) withdraws this immunity if the intermediary, upon receiving "actual knowledge" via a court order or
government notification that any information/data/link is being used to commit an unlawful act, fails to
expeditiously remove or disable access to it.
Key Issues
• Ambiguity of "actual knowledge": Shreya Singhal (2015) held it means a court/government order — not private
complaints — yet informal takedown pressure persists outside this standard.
• "Expeditiously" undefined: no precise statutory timeline (distinct from the 36-hr rule under IT Rules), causing inconsistent
enforcement.
• Privatised censorship: fear of losing safe harbour drives over-removal of lawful content ("collateral censorship").
• Conflicts with encryption: Rule 4(2) traceability mandate for the "first originator" undermines end-to-end encryption
(WhatsApp's pending Delhi HC challenge).
• Broad, open-ended due-diligence obligations (Rule 3) burden smaller intermediaries/start-ups, favouring large
incumbents.
• No judicial-determination requirement: mere government notifications (not always court-backed) can trigger loss of
immunity — due process concerns.
• Intermediaries effectively perform a quasi-judicial adjudicatory role on content legality without legal expertise or
transparency.

In an era where digital media has become the new public square, the challenge is not merely of regulation, but of
preservation with prudence. While the unbridled flow of information empowers democracy, unchecked content can just
as swiftly undermine it through hate, misinformation, and manipulation. Yet, regulation must not become a cloak for
censorship, nor should the fear of misuse justify the stifling of dissent.
Ultimately, the goal must be to foster a digital ecosystem that is as accountable as it is open, as secure as it is plural,
where freedom of speech is not a casualty of regulation, but its most protected outcome.
IT Rules, 2021 (as amended) — Broader Issues
Framework
• Notified under Sections 69A, 79, and 87 of the IT Act; mandate due diligence for intermediaries, a three-tier
grievance redressal mechanism for digital news/OTT platforms, a Code of Ethics, and additional obligations
(traceability, proactive monitoring, grievance officers) for Significant Social Media Intermediaries (SSMIs).
• Rule 3(1)(d): intermediaries must remove/disable access to flagged unlawful content within 36 hours (24 hours in
the original 2021 text) of actual knowledge via court/government order.
Key Issues
• Executive overreach: Fact Check Unit (Rule 3(1)(b)(v)) makes govt sole arbiter of "fake/false" content about itself —
challenged in Kunal Kamra v. UOI as violative of Art. 14 & 19(1)(a).
• Vague terms — "patently false", "misleading" — lack objective standards, risking arbitrary, selective enforcement.
• Delegated legislation exceeding parent Act: Rules (subordinate instrument) impose substantive speech restrictions
not contemplated by Ss. 69A/79 — ultra vires concerns.
• Level-III grievance body (Inter-Departmental Committee) gives the executive final appellate authority over digital
news/OTT content — absent in print/broadcast media regulation.
• No prior parliamentary debate/pre-legislative consultation despite wide impact on press freedom and digital
businesses.
• Heavy compliance burden (resident grievance officers, periodic reports) chills smaller platforms/digital media.
• Traceability (Rule 4(2)) vs. privacy/encryption trade-off conflicts with K.S. Puttaswamy (2017) right to privacy.
• Overlaps with DPDP Act 2023, Competition Act, BNS misinformation provisions — fragmented compliance regime.
• Global coordination gap: cross-border platforms face conflicting obligations vis-à-vis EU DSA/US Sec. 230.
• Frequent ad-hoc amendments (2022, 2023; proposed 2025 deepfake/AI-labelling rules) create regulatory
unpredictability.
Way Forward
• Enact a principle-based Digital India Act (replacing the 2000-
era IT Act) with clear, narrowly-tailored definitions and built-in
judicial safeguards.
• Strengthen Section 69A safeguards — mandatory reasoned
orders, post-decisional hearing, independent (non-executive)
review, per Shreya Singhal.
• Codify "actual knowledge" and "expeditious" timelines
precisely under 79(3)(b) to curb ambiguity and over-removal.
• Replace the government-run Fact Check Unit with an
independent, multi-stakeholder fact-checking body.
• Risk-based, graded due-diligence obligations — lighter for
small intermediaries, stricter for SSMIs.
• Protect end-to-end encryption; explore judicially-supervised
alternatives to blanket traceability.
Institutionalise pre-legislative consultation
