Issues and Criticisms
1.Reversal of burden of proof: A person on the voter list is presumed a citizen (Lal Babu Hussein v. Electoral Registration Officer
(1995) unless the State proves otherwise; the SIR instead required voters not on the 2002/03 legacy rolls to prove citizenship but
through specified documents.
2.Discriminatory documentation burden: Stringent document requirements (pre-1987 documents, parental birth certificates)
disproportionately burden Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, minorities, migrant workers, the poor, the illiterate, and women
who migrated after marriage.Historically low birth-registration rates compound this — >only 25% of births were registered in 2007,
the birth year of those who turned 18 by 2025.
3.Compressed timelines close to elections: Courts and critics questioned why the exercise was conducted so close to the Assembly
election, and why Aadhaar was initially excluded from the list of admissible documents.
4.ECI overreach into citizenship questions: Critics, including opposition leaders, contended the exercise effectively functions as a
"backdoor NRC," with the ECI encroaching on the Home Ministry's constitutional domain over citizenship determination, since
intensive electoral roll revisions shift the burden of proof of citizenship onto individuals with no clear legal framework to determine
their status, potentially pushing them into a state of permanent suspicion.
5.Assam precedent as cautionary tale: In Assam, a past SIR led to "doubtful voters" facing Foreigners' Tribunals whose functioning
has been arbitrary; in December 2025, a Nagaon resident was pushed into Bangladesh despite due-process concerns, with Tribunal
member qualifications having weakened over time (from mandatory retired judges in 1964 to any lawyer with seven years'
experience today).
6.Data disputes and administrative errors: During hearings, contradictory claims emerged over the scale of deletions — with
instances cited of persons declared dead who were found alive, and vice versa — while the ECI maintained 7.24 crore of 7.9 crore
voters had responded to the exercise, disputing claims of a crore or more being disenfranchised.
7.Administrative strain: The compressed exercise has been described as increasingly exclusionary, opaque, and burdensome, with
several BLO deaths reported elsewhere due to unrealistic deadlines and workload pressure, and legacy rolls from 2002–04 poorly
digitised and difficult to access.
8.Institutional design gap: Deeper issue is one of unchecked institutional power — the ECI's own appointment process (post the
2023 CEC and Other ECs Act, replacing the CJI with a Union Cabinet Minister on the selection panel, itself under challenge in Jaya
Thakur v. Union of India) raises questions about independent oversight over an exercise with such profound electoral
consequences.
9.Post-facto judicial review: Since the verdict came six months after the Bihar polls concluded, judicial correction — however
emphatic — could not undo any wrongful exclusions that may have affected the actual vote.
The SIR process has been conducted in more than 15 states so far and approx 5 crore names from the voters list have been removed.

Global Best Practices in Electoral Roll Maintenance
Continuous/automatic voter registration (e.g., many European democracies, parts of the US): Rolls updated in real time
through linkage with civil registration (birth/death) and residency databases, avoiding periodic disruptive "intensive"
exercises.
UK: Rolling annual canvass with individual electoral registration (IER), supplemented by data-matching against government
records rather than blanket re-verification.
Canada: National Register of Electors updated continuously via linkages with tax, immigration, and vital statistics agencies
— voters need not "re-prove" eligibility periodically.
Germany: Registration is near-automatic via the mandatory municipal residence registration (Meldewesen) system.
International principle (UN Human Rights Committee, General Comment 25): Any restriction on the right to vote must be
based on objective, reasonable criteria, with adequate safeguards, including a fair hearing before removal — informing the
debate on whether SIR's timelines and documentary demands meet this due-process threshold.
Common thread: mature democracies minimise burden-shifting onto citizens by relying on inter-agency data integration
rather than repeated door-to-door documentary re-verification
Way Forward
1.Institutionalise continuous roll updation via linkage (with privacy safeguards) to civil registration systems (birth/death
registers), reducing the need for periodic disruptive SIRs.
2.Restore and codify the Lal Babu Hussein presumption: Any exclusion of an already-enrolled elector should require
individualised inquiry and a reasoned, appealable order — not a blanket document-production mandate.
3.Decouple SIR timing from elections: Statutory cooling-off periods (e.g., no intensive revision within X months of a
scheduled election) to prevent last-minute disenfranchisement risk and allow adequate time for claims/objections and
appeals.
4.Widen and simplify the documentary regime: Universal acceptance of Aadhaar/other widely-held IDs from the outset,
with alternative proof mechanisms (community verification, sworn affidavits with penalty for false claims) for those
without legacy documents, particularly for SC/ST, migrant, and women voters.
5.Strengthen appellate infrastructure: Adequately staffed, judicially trained appellate tribunals with realistic case-load
timelines, distinct from the executive-controlled Foreigners' Tribunal model that has drawn criticism in Assam.
6.Clarify institutional boundaries: Explicit statutory clarification that the ECI's SIR is confined to roll eligibility and
cannot be used as a proxy citizenship-determination exercise; any citizenship doubt must be routed through due
process under the Citizenship Act.
7.Independent, multi-partisan ECI appointments: Resolving the pending challenge in Jaya Thakur and restoring a broad-
based selection process would strengthen public trust in exercises as consequential as the SIR.
8.Transparency by design: Real-time, searchable, reasoned deletion databases (as directed for Bihar) should become a
standard, pre-emptive feature of every SIR, not a court-ordered afterthought.
9.Time-bound judicial review: Given the Bihar experience of a verdict arriving six months post-election, courts and
legislature should consider expedited/interim review mechanisms so that constitutional questions around a state's
electoral roll are resolved before, not after, the relevant election

In 2026 the Government introduced a three-bill package to break this deadlock:
● Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026 — sought to delete the constitutional stipulation (in the Article 82 proviso) that the next
delimitation must follow the first Census after 2026, thereby permitting delimitation to proceed using 2011 Census data rather than waiting

● Delimitation Bill, 2026 — would have repealed and replaced the Delimitation Act, 2002, and empowered the Union Government to

● Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026 — extended equivalent changes to Delhi, Puducherry and Jammu & Kashmir.

stabilisation, while rewarding higher-fertility northern states with a larger seat share.
This effectively re-opened India's long-standing federal fault line over population-based representation, with women's reservation
caught in the middle of it.

Issues and Challenges
Implementation and Design Issues
Delimitation deadlock: Tying operationalisation to a future delimitation
exercise has, in practice, deferred the reservation by a decade or more,
undermining the credibility of a constitutional guarantee that enjoyed
near-unanimous political support in 2023.
Federal tension over delimitation: Any attempt to expedite delimitation
using updated Census data risks reopening the population-versus-
representation debate, since states with better demographic performance
(mostly southern) fear a relative loss of seats and political weight — as
seen in the defeat of the 131st Amendment Bill, 2026.
Rotation of reserved constituencies: As with Panchayats, rotating
reserved seats after each delimitation cycle may discourage sustained
constituency-building by women MPs/MLAs and create uncertainty for
incumbents and parties alike.
15-year sunset clause: Article 334A limits reservation to 15 years unless
extended, raising questions about whether one delimitation cycle is
sufficient to institutionalise change before the clause potentially lapses.
No sub-quota for OBC women: Unlike the SC/ST sub-quota built into the
Act, there is no reservation for OBC women, a long-standing demand
from several parties that was a major reason earlier versions of the Bill
(1996–2010) repeatedly failed.
Structural and Political Issues
Party ticket distribution: Even without reservation, parties could
voluntarily field more women candidates; the 2024 elections show most
major parties gave women well under 20% of tickets, indicating limited
voluntary commitment absent a legal mandate.
Proxy representation risk: The Panchayat-level experience with proxy
control by male relatives ("sarpanch pati"/"pradhan pati") raises a real risk
of similar dynamics at the state and national level unless accompanied by
political and social empowerment measures.
Intra-party glass ceiling: Even where women are elected, their presence in
Cabinet, key ministries, and party leadership positions remains
disproportionately low relative to their numbers in the House.
Resource and campaign disadvantages: Women candidates often face
greater financial constraints, less access to party campaign machinery, and
social/safety barriers to sustained campaigning, especially in rural
constituencies.
Intersectionality: Reservation calculated purely on gender may still under-
represent women from religious minorities, disability groups, or other
marginalised intersections not captured by the SC/ST sub-quota alone.

1.Pre-Independence Roots
a.Nationalist parties like the Indian National Congress (INC, 1885) and the All India Muslim League (1906) began
as elite associations but gradually massified, transforming from pressure groups into proto-parties that
negotiated with the colonial state.
b.Example (mass mobilisation): The Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience movements under Congress
showed how a party could mobilise peasants, workers, students and the middle class around a shared political
programme.
c.Case study: The Lahore Session of INC (1929) resolved to demand Poorna Swaraj and launched a coordinated
political campaign—an early example of a party articulating a coherent national agenda.
2.Phase I – One-Party Dominant “Congress System” (1952–1967)
a.India started as a multi-party system in law, but in practice saw a Congress-dominant system, with INC winning
comfortable majorities in the first four general elections.
b.Known as the “Congress system”, where Congress functioned as an umbrella party representing diverse social
coalitions and factions internally rather than through multiple parties.
c.Example: In the 1957 Lok Sabha, INC won ~75% of seats, while opposition parties were fragmented and
regionally limited.
d.Global comparison: Similar to the Liberal Democratic Party (LDP) dominance in post-war Japan, where one
party repeatedly won power in a formally competitive multi-party setting.
3.Phase II – Fragmentation & Rise of Regional Parties (1967–1989)
a.The 1967 election broke Congress’s invincibility; coalition governments emerged in several states and regional
parties (DMK in Tamil Nadu, Akali Dal in Punjab, etc.) gained prominence.
b.The Emergency (1975–77) and the Janata Party experiment (1977–79) showed the potential and fragility of
broad anti-Congress coalitions.
c.Example: The 1977 Lok Sabha election—Congress was swept out of power in North India; the Janata Party, a
coalition of several opposition groups, formed the first non-Congress Union government.
4.Phase III – Coalition Era & Multi-Party Federalisation (1989–2014)
a.From 1989 to 2014, India saw unstable minority governments, broad coalitions (United Front, NDA, UPA) and
growing influence of regional parties in coalition bargaining at the Centre.
b.Federalisation of the party system meant state-level parties (DMK, TDP, JD(U), Shiv Sena, TMC, etc.) became
king-makers in Delhi.
c.Example: UPA-I (2004–09) relied on outside support from the Left Front, which used this leverage to influence
policies like withdrawal from the original Indo-US nuclear deal configuration.
5.Phase IV – A New Dominant-Party Pattern (2014–present)
a.Since 2014, the BJP has emerged as the new dominant party at the national level, winning clear majorities in
2014 and 2019 and remaining the single largest party in 2024, even as regional parties retain strong state
bases.
b.India today is best described as a “multi-party, asymmetrically competitive” system—many parties exist, but one
party has a clear structural advantage.
c.Example: In the 2024 Lok Sabha elections, six national parties together secured over 63% of the total votes,
with BJP remaining the largest party.

2.Criminalisation of Politics
a.A significant share of MPs and MLAs across parties have serious criminal cases.

India's party system is unique in scale and diversity: from a phase of “one-party dominance” under the Congress (1952–67), through the rise of
opposition and regional assertion (1967–89), to the era of coalition politics (1989–2014), and now a phase some scholars call the “second

Dynamics Between National and Regional Parties
Coalition Bargaining: Since 1989, regional parties have repeatedly held the balance of power at the Centre,
extracting policy concessions (special category status demands, central fund allocation, portfolio bargains)
— a dynamic visible again in 2024 with TDP and JD(U) as key NDA partners.
Centre–State Friction: Regional parties in opposition-ruled states frequently clash with the Centre over
fiscal federalism (GST compensation, devolution formula under Finance Commission), Governor's conduct,
and use of central agencies (ED, CBI) — seen in disputes involving Tamil Nadu, West Bengal, Kerala, and
Punjab.
Absorption and Realignment: National parties often co-opt regional leaders/parties (e.g., splinters of Shiv
Sena and NCP joining NDA in Maharashtra, 2022–23) — reshaping the very distinction between “national”
and “regional.”
Vote-Bank Consolidation: Regional parties often build sub-national coalitions around caste/community
blocs (Yadav-Muslim in RJD/SP, Maratha in Shiv Sena/NCP), which can override programmatic politics.
Symbol and Identity Politics: Party symbol disputes following splits (Shiv Sena, NCP, 2022–23) illustrate
how the ECI's adjudicative role under the 1968 Order has become a site of intense political contestation.
National Front Formations: Regional parties periodically federate into national-level fronts (National Front
1989, United Front 1996, INDIA bloc 2023) to counterbalance a dominant national party — reflecting the
“federalisation” of Indian party politics without full nationalisation of any single alternative party.

Key Issues
1. Criminalisation of Politics: Rising proportion of MPs/MLAs facing criminal charges across both national and regional
parties; weak enforcement of disqualification despite Lily Thomas v. Union of India (2013) and Public Interest
Foundation v. UoI (2018) directions on disclosure.
2. Dynastic and Personality-Centred Structures: Many regional parties are effectively family enterprises (DMK, SP, RJD,
NCP, Shiv Sena, TDP, JD(S)), undermining inner-party democracy and long-term institutionalisation.
3. Opaque Political Funding: The Electoral Bond Scheme (2018–2024) enabled anonymous, unlimited corporate
donations; struck down by the Supreme Court in Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India (Feb 2024) for
violating Article 19(1)(a) (voters' right to information) — yet the underlying problem of opaque funding to both national
and regional parties persists through electoral trusts and unreported cash.
4. Weak Anti-Defection Enforcement: Speaker's discretion, absence of time limits, and politically motivated delays
(Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Speaker, Manipur LA, 2020) have enabled engineered defections that topple state
governments — Karnataka (2019), Madhya Pradesh (2020), Maharashtra (2022) — disproportionately affecting regional-
party-led coalitions.
5. Party Fragmentation and Coalition Instability: A high number of registered-unrecognised parties (2,800+) alongside
declining vote-share thresholds for recognition dilutes the meaningfulness of “national party” status; frequent splits
fragment regional mandates.
6. Regionalism vs National Cohesion: Excessive sub-nationalism can produce inter-state disputes (Cauvery, Mullaperiyar,
language imposition debates) and complicate national policy coordination (GST Council consensus, NEP rollout).
7. ECI's Limited Regulatory Teeth: No statutory power to deregister a party for constitutional violations (only the Model
Code of Conduct, which lacks legal backing); Section 29A registration is largely a formality with no substantive vetting of
internal democracy.
8. Erosion of Ideology in Favour of Patronage: Both national and regional parties increasingly compete on welfare
populism (“freebies”) and identity mobilisation rather than programmatic policy differentiation, raising concerns flagged
by the Supreme Court in S. Subramaniam Balaji v. Government of Tamil Nadu (2013).
Way Forward / Reform Recommendations
1. Statutory Backing for the Model Code of Conduct: Convert MCC into a legally enforceable code with graded penalties,
reducing selective/delayed enforcement against both national and regional ruling parties.
2. Mandatory Inner-Party Democracy: Amend Section 29A registration norms to require periodic, ECI-audited internal
elections and transparent leadership succession — addressing dynastic capture common in regional parties.
3. State Funding of Elections / Reformed Donation Regime: Building on the Indrajit Gupta Committee (1998)
recommendation and the 2024 Electoral Bonds verdict, introduce a transparent, capped, and traceable funding mechanism
(e.g., a National Election Fund) applicable uniformly to national and regional parties.
4. Independent Tribunal for Anti-Defection Adjudication: Implement the Keisham Meghachandra Singh (2020) suggestion
for a time-bound, judicially supervised mechanism (retired SC/HC judge-headed tribunal) to replace Speaker discretion,
reducing engineered defections that most often destabilise regional-party governments.
5. Rationalised Recognition Criteria: Periodically review ECI's vote-share/seat thresholds for national/state party status to
prevent both excessive fragmentation and unfair loss of recognition for genuinely representative regional parties.
6. Strengthening Cooperative Federalism Platforms: Institutionalise forums like the Inter-State Council and empower the
GST Council model of consensus-building to channel regional party demands constructively rather than adversarially.
7. Regulating Political Advertising and Populism: Following S. Subramaniam Balaji (2013), develop clearer guidelines
(possibly via ECI-issued fiscal responsibility disclosures) for welfare promises made by both national and regional parties
during campaigns.
8. Decriminalisation of Politics: Fast-track courts for MP/MLA criminal cases (as directed by the Supreme Court in Public
Interest Foundation, 2018) to ensure timely disposal before the next election cycle, applicable uniformly across party lines.

Issues
1. Post-facto nature of audit: CAG audits after expenditure has occurred, limiting real-time correction of financial mismanagement.
2. Delayed tabling of reports: Reports often laid before Parliament/Assemblies months or years after the audit period, diluting relevance and
public salience.
3. Limited follow-up mechanism: No binding power to enforce recommendations; depends entirely on PAC and executive responsiveness.
4. Coverage gaps: Private-public partnerships (PPPs), autonomous societies, and NGOs receiving government funds often fall outside effective
audit reach despite huge public money flows.
5. Capacity constraints: Shortage of trained personnel for specialised sectors like defence procurement, IT systems, and complex financial
instruments (derivatives, disinvestment valuations).
6. Political weaponisation of reports: Findings (e.g., 2G, Coal block allocation) often get reduced to political slugfests rather than triggering
systemic correction.
7. Appointment process opacity: CAG is appointed solely by the executive (President on PM's advice) without a broad-based collegium —
raising independence concerns, unlike some peer democracies.
8. State-level neglect: State CAG reports often receive far less legislative scrutiny than Union reports due to weaker PAC functioning at state
level.
9. No pre-audit for major contracts: Confined to post-audit; India lacks strong ex-ante financial control comparable to some OECD SAIs.
1. 2G Spectrum Scam (2010): CAG report flagged arbitrary, first-come-first-served allocation of telecom spectrum without auction, estimating
massive presumptive loss to the exchequer — led to Supreme Court cancellation of licences and triggered major reforms in resource allocation
via auction.
2. Coal Block Allocation ("Coalgate", 2012): CAG highlighted opaque allocation of coal blocks without competitive bidding — Supreme Court
subsequently cancelled over 200 allocations (2014).
3. Commonwealth Games 2010: CAG audit uncovered cost overruns, procurement irregularities, and inflated contracts, feeding into PAC
investigations and prosecutions.
4. Rafale Deal Audit (2019): CAG's report on defence procurement examined pricing benchmarks, illustrating the CAG's expanding but
contested role in auditing sensitive defence contracts.
5. Ayushman Bharat / State scheme audits: Performance audits of social sector schemes have flagged beneficiary duplication, fund diversion,
and implementation gaps at state level.

Reform Recommendations (Way Forward)
1. Institutionalise real-time/concurrent audit: Move beyond post-facto audit for large infrastructure and defence contracts to enable timely
correction.
2. Mandatory Action Taken Reports (ATRs): Legally binding timelines for ministries/departments to respond to CAG findings, similar to
GAO's High-Risk List follow-up model.
3. Broaden appointment process: Constitute a broad-based collegium (including Chief Justice, Leader of Opposition) for CAG appointment

4. Expand audit jurisdiction: Bring PPPs, large NGOs, and autonomous bodies receiving substantial public funds more comprehensively
within audit ambit.
5. Strengthen technical capacity: Recruit domain specialists (forensic accountants, IT auditors, defence procurement experts) rather than
relying solely on generalist IAAS cadre.
6. Depoliticise report handling: Encourage a culture of using CAG findings for systemic correction rather than partisan point-scoring —
possible via structured, non-adversarial PAC hearings.
7. Strengthen state-level PAC functioning: Capacity-building and staffing support for State PACs to ensure CAG state reports receive
adequate legislative scrutiny.
8. Leverage technology and data analytics: Use AI-driven anomaly detection for large-scale financial data (as GAO and NAO increasingly
do) to flag irregularities faster and more accurately.
The CAG is not merely the government’s accountant, but democracy’s financial conscience—ensuring that every rupee raised in the people’s name

Core Changes Introduced
A. Preambular and Ideological Changes
• Inserted the words "Socialist," "Secular," and "Integrity" into the Preamble, changing "Sovereign Democratic Republic" to "Sovereign
Socialist Secular Democratic Republic."
B. Fundamental Duties
• Inserted a new Part IV-A (Article 51A), prescribing ten Fundamental Duties for citizens (respect for the Constitution, the National
Flag and Anthem, protection of the environment, scientific temper, etc.), drawing inspiration from the erstwhile USSR's constitutional
model.
C. Directive Principles vs. Fundamental Rights
• Amended Article 31C to give DPSPs under all of Part IV (not merely Articles 39(b) and (c)) primacy over Fundamental Rights under
Articles 14, 19, and 31, and barred judicial review of laws framed to implement any Directive Principle.
• Added Article 39A (equal justice and free legal aid) and Article 43A (workers' participation in management).
D. Curtailment of Judicial Review
• Article 32A: Barred the Supreme Court from considering the constitutional validity of state laws in writ petitions under Article 32.
• Article 131A: Gave the Supreme Court exclusive jurisdiction over questions on the validity of Central laws, excluding High Courts.
• Article 144A: Required a minimum number of judges and a two-thirds majority for the SC to decide on the validity of a Central law.
• Article 226A: Barred High Courts from considering the constitutional validity of Central laws in writ proceedings.
• Article 228A: Similar restriction on High Courts regarding the validity of state laws, requiring reference to a larger bench.
• Diluted Articles 226/227 by narrowing the grounds on which High Courts could issue writs and curtailing their power of
superintendence over subordinate courts and tribunals.
E. Amending Power of Parliament
• Article 368(4): Declared that no constitutional amendment could be questioned in any court on any ground.
• Article 368(5): Declared there was no limitation whatsoever on Parliament's constituent power to amend, vary, or repeal any
provision of the Constitution — a direct attempt to nullify the Basic Structure doctrine laid down in Kesavananda Bharati.
F. Centre–State Relations
• Enabled the Union to deploy central armed forces to any state to deal with law and order without the state's consent (Article 257A,
later omitted).
• Extended the President's power under emergency provisions and facilitated greater central legislative competence, tilting the
federal balance toward the Centre.
G. Tenure of Legislatures and Elections
• Extended the term of the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies from 5 to 6 years.
• Extended the life of the Lok Sabha itself (elected in 1971) by one year through a Proclamation of Emergency, postponing the 1976
general election.
H. Administrative and Miscellaneous Changes
• Shifted subjects like Education, Forests, Weights and Measures, and Protection of Wild Animals and Birds from the State List to the
Concurrent List (7th Schedule), expanding Parliament's legislative reach.
• Introduced provisions enabling creation of an All India Judicial Service.
• Added Articles 323A and 323B, enabling the establishment of Administrative Tribunals to reduce the burden on High Courts (this
specific provision survived and was operationalised via the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985).
• Made the President explicitly bound by the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (Article 74(1)) — retained even after 1978.
Dimension 	Impact
Executive–Judiciary balance Sharply tilted power toward the executive by insulating constitutional amendments and
DPSP-based laws from judicial review
Federalism 	Centralised legislative and administrative power by expanding the Concurrent List
Fundamental Rights 	Diminished the primacy of Articles 14, 19 and 31 relative to Directive Principles,
Citizen responsibility Introduced, for the first time, a codified sense of civic obligation (Fundamental Duties),
shifting political discourse to include duties alongside rights.
Democratic process 	Undermined electoral accountability by postponing elections and extending legislative
tenure — seen as one of the most authoritarian features of the Emergency era.
Long-term jurisprudence
Provoked the strongest judicial push-back in independent India's history (Minerva Mills,
1980), cementing the Basic Structure doctrine as an entrenched limit on Parliament's
amending power for all future amendments.

Refers to the idea that a Constitution is not merely a static legal document but a dynamic instrument meant to
transform society by dismantling entrenched inequalities and hierarchies.
Intro
Coined initially in the South African constitutional discourse, the term gained prominence in Indian jurisprudence
post the 2010s. Indian courts have used it to interpret the Constitution as a living document that evolves to meet
the needs of justice, dignity, and equality.
Constitutional Provision 	Transformative Implication
Preamble Establishes justice, liberty, equality, and fraternity as core values aimed at
transforming Indian society
Part III (Fundamental Rights)
Empowers citizens and restrains state power; abolishes untouchability
(Art. 17), guarantees equality (Art. 14), freedom (Art. 19), and life with
dignity (Art. 21)
Part IV (DPSPs) Articulates socio-economic goals — right to education, health, living wage,
etc. — directing the State to actively work towards justice and welfare
Article 32 & 226 Judicial review and constitutional remedies ensure enforcement of rights
and corrective interpretation by the courts
Constitutional Foundations of Transformative Constitutionalism
Key Dimensions of Transformative Constitutionalism in India
1.Social Transformation
a.Abolition of Social Hierarchies:
b.Constitution (Article 17) abolished untouchability.
c.Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala (2018) (Sabarimala case): SC held that exclusion of women based on
menstruation violates Articles 14, 15, and 25.
d.Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018): Decriminalised homosexuality by striking down Section 377 IPC, affirming the
right to dignity and identity of LGBTQ+ persons.
2.Caste and Reservation:
a.Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992): Upheld OBC reservations but introduced the creamy layer concept to ensure social
justice.
b.Jarnail Singh v. Lachhmi Narain Gupta (2018): Extended creamy layer concept to SC/ST promotions.
c.State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh (2024) : States can sub categorise on the basis of caste
3.Gender Justice
a.Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997): Laid down guidelines to prevent sexual harassment at workplace using international
conventions in the absence of domestic law.
b.Joseph Shine v. Union of India (2018): Decriminalized adultery (Section 497 IPC), holding it discriminatory against women.
4.Environmental Justice
a.M.C. Mehta Cases: Judicial activism led to the polluter pays principle, environmental impact assessments, and the right to
clean air and water being read into Article 21.
5.Economic and Labour Rights
a.Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985): Right to livelihood held intrinsic to the right to life (Art. 21).
b.Workmen v. Meenakshi Mills Ltd. (1992): Affirmed workers' right to fair treatment and collective bargaining.
c.PUCL v. Union of India (2001 onwards): Led to the recognition of the right to food and the formulation of schemes like
MGNREGA and midday meals.
6.Recognition of Intersectional and Emerging Rights
a.K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017):
b.Recognized right to privacy as intrinsic to life and liberty under Article 21.
c.Laid foundation for data protection, bodily autonomy, and sexual identity rights.

Key Dimensions
1.Rule of Law: Upholding supremacy of law and ensuring equality before the law.
2.Democratic Governance: Ensuring participatory democracy and accountability of governmental institutions.
3.Fundamental Rights Protection: Safeguarding individual rights against arbitrary state action.
4.Social Justice and Equality: Addressing historical discrimination and promoting substantive equality.
5.Judicial Integrity and Independence: Courts act impartially, free from political influences, safeguarding democratic
values.
6.Judicial Interpretations
Landmark Judgments and Case Studies
1.Naz Foundation v. Govt. of NCT of Delhi (2009): The Delhi High Court invoked constitutional morality to strike
down Section 377 of IPC, highlighting the necessity of protecting minority rights irrespective of societal morality.
2.Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018): The Supreme Court reaffirmed constitutional morality, overturning the
reinstatement of Section 377 by the earlier Koushal judgment (2013), underscoring dignity, liberty, and equality of
the LGBTQ+ community.
3.Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala (Sabarimala Case, 2018): Emphasizing constitutional morality
over popular sentiment, the Supreme Court allowed entry of women into the Sabarimala Temple, thereby
upholding gender equality.
4.Joseph Shine v. Union of India (2018): Decriminalizing adultery, the Court stressed constitutional morality in terms
of gender equality and individual autonomy.
5.KS Puttaswamy v. Union of India (Privacy Case, 2017): Upheld privacy as a fundamental right, invoking
constitutional morality in protecting individual autonomy and dignity.
Criticisms and Challenges
1.Ambiguity and Judicial Subjectivity: Critics argue constitutional morality is open-ended, potentially leading to
judicial overreach and subjective interpretations.
2.Conflict with Popular Morality: Implementation challenges arise when constitutional morality opposes societal
beliefs, as seen in Sabarimala, causing widespread societal and political resistance.
3.Significance and Impact
4.Constitutional morality has reinforced India's commitment to transformative constitutionalism, promoting social
reform and justice.
5.It fosters inclusive democracy by prioritizing constitutional ethics over majoritarian impulses.
6.Encourages progressive societal attitudes, protecting minorities and marginalised communities.
Way Forward
Clear judicial articulation and consistent application can mitigate ambiguity.
Broader societal education and dialogue can align societal values closer to constitutional ideals.
Strengthening institutional frameworks to operationalise constitutional morality more effectively.
Conclusion
Constitutional morality acts as a compass guiding democratic governance towards justice, liberty, and equality. Its
significance lies in transcending transient societal prejudices to uphold enduring constitutional values. Continued
emphasis on constitutional morality is essential to realizing India's transformative constitutional vision of a just,
equitable, and inclusive society.
