Statutory Framework
Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987
Enacted to give statutory teeth to Article 39A, the Act came into force on 9 November 1995 (chosen as Legal Services
Day/National Legal Services Day). It constitutes a nationwide, tiered network of legal services authorities and mandates the
organisation of Lok Adalats for amicable dispute settlement.
• Section 12: Lays down the criteria of eligibility for free legal services — discussed in Section 5 (Beneficiaries) below.
• Sections 19–22: Provide for the organisation of Lok Adalats and the legal status of their awards, which are deemed decrees of
a civil court, final, binding, and not appealable.
• Section 22A–22E (inserted by the 2002 Amendment): Provide for Permanent Lok Adalats for pre-litigation conciliation and
settlement of disputes relating to public utility services (transport, postal, telegraph, electricity, water supply, hospitals,
insurance, etc.).
• 1994 & 2002 Amendments: Broadened the scope of legal aid, introduced Permanent Lok Adalats, and refined the institutional
hierarchy.
Allied Statutory and Policy Instruments
• Section 341, BNSS: mandates State-provided pleader for an accused who cannot afford one in trials before a Court of Session.
• The NALSA (Free and Competent Legal Services) Regulations, 2010: prescribe norms for quality, competence and monitoring
of legal aid counsel.
• Model Rules under the Act framed by each State to operationalise SLSAs, DLSAs and Taluk Legal Services Committees.
• Tele-Law Scheme (2017, Department of Justice) and Nyaya Bandhu (Pro Bono Legal Services) programme, integrating legal aid
with e-governance and pro bono practice by advocates and law students.
Beneficiaries: Who Is Entitled to Free Legal Aid?
Section 12 of the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 :
• Members of a Scheduled Caste or Scheduled Tribe.
• Victims of trafficking in human beings or of begar (forced labour), as referred to in Article 23.
• Women and children (including women victims of crime and child victims/witnesses).
• Persons with disabilities, as defined under applicable disability legislation.
• Persons under circumstances of undeserved want such as victims of a mass disaster, ethnic violence, caste atrocity, flood,
drought, earthquake or industrial disaster.
• Industrial workmen.
• Persons in custody, including in a protective home, juvenile home, psychiatric hospital or nursing home.
• Persons whose annual income is below the limit prescribed by the respective State Government (a threshold periodically
revised; several States and the Supreme Court Legal Services Committee currently place the limit in the range of roughly Rs 3–5
lakh per annum, with variation across States).
• Persons in cases before a Labour Court, Industrial Tribunal or other labour authority, and litigants in Lok Adalat or mediation
proceedings.

Dispute Resolution Arms

Issues and Challenges
• Poor Quality of Legal Representation: Legal aid lawyers are often overburdened, underpaid (fee schedules significantly below
market rates), and in some cases inadequately experienced, leading to a perception of legal aid as a symbolic rather than
substantive right.
• Low Public Awareness: A large proportion of eligible beneficiaries, particularly in rural and tribal areas, remain unaware that free
legal aid exists or of the procedure to access it.
• Inadequate Funding: NALSA and SLSAs face persistent budgetary constraints, restricting the number of empanelled lawyers,
PLVs, and outreach camps that can be sustained.
• Understaffing and Vacancies: Chronic vacancies in DLSA secretary posts and shortage of trained panel lawyers, especially in
remote and left-wing-extremism-affected districts.
• Coordination Gaps: Overlapping mandates and uneven coordination between DLSAs, police, prisons, and the judiciary can delay
identification of eligible undertrials.
• Quality vs Quantity Trade-off in Lok Adalats: The emphasis on high disposal numbers in Lok Adalats has drawn criticism that
settlements are sometimes achieved through pressure to compromise rather than genuine consent, particularly on the weaker
party.
• Digital Divide: Tele-Law and e-services depend on connectivity and digital literacy, which remain uneven across rural India,
limiting the reach of digitally delivered legal aid.
• Gender and Social Barriers: Stigma, lack of mobility, and patriarchal control can prevent women and marginalised groups from
approaching legal aid clinics despite formal entitlement.
• Weak Monitoring and Evaluation: Limited independent, outcome-based evaluation of whether legal aid actually improves case
outcomes for beneficiaries, as opposed to merely providing token representation.
Undertrial Backlog: A large share of India's prison population continues to be undertrials, many entitled to free legal aid and bail
assistance that is not always timely accessed
Best Practices — Domestic and Global
• Nyaya Bandhu and Tele-Law: Integration of legal aid with digital platforms and Common Service Centres to extend reach into
rural India.
• Legal Literacy Clubs in Schools: NALSA's scheme to build legal awareness among students, nurturing a rights-conscious
citizenry from a young age.
• Village Legal Care and Support Centres: Grassroots-level clinics run jointly with Panchayati Raj Institutions to bring legal aid
closer to where people live.
• Prison Legal Aid Clinics: Dedicated clinics within jails to assist undertrials with bail applications and case status, reducing
pretrial detention.
United States (Public Defender System) : Constitutionally mandated (Gideon v. Wainwright, 1963) right to counsel in criminal
matters, delivered through dedicated public defender offices in most jurisdictions.
Netherlands : A mixed model combining a public Legal Aid Board with subsidised private practitioners, alongside ‘law shops’
for early-stage legal advice.
Finland : Universal public legal aid offices staffed by salaried lawyers, with income-linked co-payment rather than a strict
eligibility cut-off, widening effective access. Way Forward
• Strengthen the Quality Assurance Framework: Move towards a cadre of salaried, full-time legal aid defence counsel on the
South African/public defender model, at least in criminal matters, supplementing the empanelled-lawyer system.
• Enhanced and Ring-Fenced Funding: Guarantee a fixed percentage of the judiciary/law ministry budget for legal aid, insulated
from routine budgetary cuts, alongside better honoraria for panel lawyers.
• Expand Para-Legal Volunteer Networks: Scale up recruitment and training of PLVs, particularly women and persons from
marginalised communities, to serve as trusted community-level intermediaries.
• Deepen Digital Outreach While Bridging the Divide: Expand Tele-Law and Nyaya Bandhu while simultaneously investing in
physical Common Service Centres and offline camps in low-connectivity regions.
• Outcome-Based Monitoring: Institute independent, periodic evaluation of legal aid outcomes (not just number of cases
handled) using justice-delivery metrics, potentially via NITI Aayog or an independent Justice Access Index.
• Proactive Identification of Beneficiaries: Mandate police and prison authorities to inform every eligible arrestee/undertrial of
their right to legal aid at the point of arrest, with documented compliance.
• Sensitisation and Legal Literacy: Scale up legal literacy clubs, village outreach camps and mass media campaigns to close the
awareness gap, especially among women, SC/ST communities and persons with disabilities.
• Safeguard Voluntariness in Lok Adalats: Build procedural safeguards to ensure that settlement in Lok Adalats reflects genuine
consent, not disposal-driven pressure, particularly protecting the economically weaker party.
• Integrate Legal Aid with Restorative Justice: Link legal aid delivery with victim compensation schemes (Section 396 BNSS) and
rehabilitation support for a holistic justice-delivery approach.

“Justice delayed is justice denied, but justice hurried without fairness is justice buried — Lok Adalats attempt to reconcile speed with consensual
fairness.”
They represent India's oldest and most widely used institutionalised Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) mechanism, rooted in indigenous traditions
of panchayat-style dispute settlement and formalised through statute to relieve the formal judiciary of its chronic case backlog.
Matters Lok Adalats CAN Take Up
• Any case pending before a court, or any matter at the
pre-litigation stage, provided the parties agree to
settlement or the presiding court is satisfied it is
suitable.
• Civil disputes: property disputes, partition suits, land
acquisition compensation.
• Matrimonial and family disputes (excluding divorce
grounds requiring adversarial proof, though maintenance
and mutual-consent matters are commonly settled).
• Motor Accident Claims Tribunal (MACT) cases — one
of the largest single categories disposed through Lok
Adalats nationally.
• Bank recovery cases, cheque bounce cases
(compoundable offences under Section 138, Negotiable
Instruments Act), and labour disputes.
• Compoundable criminal offences under the Bharatiya
Nyaya Sanhita / Code of Criminal Procedure framework.
• Public utility service disputes, exclusively before
Permanent Lok Adalats.
Matters EXCLUDED from Lok Adalat Jurisdiction
• Non-compoundable criminal offences (e.g., murder,
rape, serious offences against the State).
• Cases involving offences committed against women,
children, or other matters where the law disallows
compromise (e.g., dowry death, certain POCSO
matters).
Disputes relating to a title to property in some
categories requiring adjudication rather than
compromise
Ordinary Lok Adalats have no independent adjudicatory
jurisdiction — they cannot impose a decision; their entire
function is to facilitate a consent-based settlement. If no
compromise is reached, the case is simply returned to the
court of origin for regular adjudication. Permanent Lok Adalats
are the sole exception, holding both conciliatory and binding
decision-making powers over public utility disputes within the
prescribed pecuniary limits.

Issues and Challenges
1. Quantity over Quality / “Assembly-line Justice”: The pressure to clear mega-volumes of cases (crores per National Lok Adalat) raises
concerns that settlements are sometimes rushed, with inadequate scrutiny of consent or the fairness of terms.
2. Coercion and Unequal Bargaining Power: Weaker parties (e.g., claimants against insurance companies or banks) may feel pressured to
accept lower settlement amounts to avoid prolonged litigation, undermining the voluntariness the mechanism assumes.
3. Limited Awareness: Rural and marginalised litigants often remain unaware of Lok Adalats, their eligibility criteria, or the finality of an
award once passed.
4. No Appeal Mechanism: While finality supports speedy disposal, the bar on appeal (Section 21) leaves an aggrieved party with only the
narrow and expensive remedy of writ jurisdiction, which may itself defeat the purpose of a quick, low-cost forum.
5. Repeat/Recurring Case Types Dominate: A large share of disposals are undefended or semi-mechanical categories (cheque bounce,
MACT, bank recovery), raising questions about how much substantive dispute resolution — as opposed to bulk clearance — is actually
occurring.
6. Resource and Capacity Constraints: DLSAs and Taluk committees are frequently under-staffed and under-funded relative to the
caseload, and depend heavily on the availability of retired judicial officers and volunteering advocates.
7. Permanent Lok Adalats Under-utilised: Awareness of the pre-litigation, compulsory conciliation route for public utility disputes remains
low compared to ordinary Lok Adalats, limiting its potential to prevent litigation before it starts.
Data and Monitoring Gaps: Independent, granular data on post-settlement compliance (i.e., whether awarded amounts are actually paid
and disputes truly resolved, not merely closed on paper) is limited
Indian Best Practices
• National Lok Adalats held on fixed dates across all court tiers (Supreme
Court to Taluk level) since 2015, often themed by subject-matter each
month, improving predictability and public outreach.
• e-Lok Adalats / Virtual Lok Adalats: Expanded access via video-
conferencing, particularly benefiting elderly, disabled, and geographically
remote litigants; adopted at scale during the pandemic and retained since.
• Jharkhand's near-total (99.95%) disposal rate in the first National Lok
Adalat of 2025 demonstrates that strong State Legal Services Authority
coordination and pre-identification of settlement-ready cases can
dramatically raise efficiency.
• Legal Aid Defence Counsel System (LADCS): A NALSA scheme
professionalising the panel of legal aid lawyers, improving the quality of
representation feeding into Lok Adalat settlements.
Mediation Act, 2023 synergy: Institutionalises mediation alongside Lok
Adalats and Permanent Lok Adalats, creating a layered ADR ecosystem
with the Mediation Council of India for standard-setting
1. Strengthen Consent Safeguards: Mandate a cooling-off period or a brief judicial/legal-aid counsel review before finalising high-value or
vulnerable-party settlements, to guard against coercive or uninformed compromise.
2. Expand Permanent Lok Adalats: Increase public awareness campaigns and raise/rationalise pecuniary limits periodically so more public
utility disputes are resolved before they enter formal litigation.
3. Institutionalise Post-Award Monitoring: Create a NALSA-led tracking mechanism for compliance with Lok Adalat awards, not merely
disposal counts, to measure real (not paper) resolution.
4. Deepen Digital Access: Scale e-Lok Adalats further with vernacular interfaces, assisted digital kiosks at Gram Nyayalayas/Panchayats, and
integration with the e-Courts platform for seamless case referral.
5. Capacity Building: Increase funding and staffing for DLSAs/Taluk Legal Services Committees; expand the pool of trained panel lawyers and
para-legal volunteers, particularly in underserved districts.
6. Targeted Outreach: Legal literacy campaigns focused on rural, tribal and economically weaker populations to improve awareness of both
ordinary and Permanent Lok Adalats as legitimate first-resort forums.
7. Data Transparency: Publish disaggregated, case-category-wise data (not just aggregate disposal figures) to allow independent assessment
of settlement quality and to guide policy calibration.
8. Harmonise with the Mediation Act, 2023: Ensure clear referral pathways between Lok Adalats, Permanent Lok Adalats, and mediation,
avoiding forum confusion and duplication for litigants.

Many pressure groups succeed by strategically combining PILs, media advocacy, and public mobilisation.
Pressure groups are the silent sentinels of democracy—powerful not because they hold office, but because they hold influence. By
amplifying diverse voices, challenging state complacency, and enriching public discourse, they transform passive citizenship into active
democracy.
Positive impact
• Democratises policymaking beyond the electoral cycle —
citizens can influence governance between elections, not just at
the ballot box.
• Brings specialised/technical knowledge into otherwise
generalist bureaucratic decision-making.
• Has driven landmark legislative outcomes: RTI Act (2005),
Forest Rights Act (2006), repeal of the three farm laws (2021).
• Strengthens civil society's watchdog function over both state
and market.
Negative impact / structural concerns
• “Demosclerosis” — a risk where a proliferation of powerful,
competing interest groups clogs governance, making coherent
policymaking difficult.
• Elite capture: Business groups (FICCI, CII, ASSOCHAM) are
often accused of disproportionately shaping economic policy
relative to labour or informal-sector interests.
• Opacity in lobbying: India has no dedicated lobbying
disclosure law, unlike several OECD democracies — creating
accountability gaps.
• Politicisation and co-option: Trade unions are frequently
affiliated with political parties (AITUC–CPI, INTUC–Congress,
diluting their independence.
• Communal/identity polarisation risk: Caste- and religion-
based groups can deepen social fault lines rather than resolve
them.
• Resource asymmetry: Grassroots/agrarian/informal groups
face chronic funding and organisational instability compared to
institutional or business groups.
• Foreign funding and regulatory friction: Repeated tightening
of the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act (FCRA) has reduced
NGO numbers and disrupted projects, raising both transparency
and civil-society-space concerns simultaneously.
Occasional resort to unconstitutional/disruptive means —
bandhs, blockades, and violence undermine the legitimacy of
otherwise democratic advocacy
Structural Challenges
1. Lack of unity and frequent factionalism within sectoral groups (e.g.,
multiple competing farmer unions).
2. Financial instability, especially for grassroots/agrarian/informal
groups.
3. Excessive dependency on and co-option by political parties,
undermining functional autonomy.
4. Absence of a dedicated lobbying transparency law.
5. Regulatory tightening (FCRA amendments) constraining NGO
operational space even as it seeks to improve accountability.
6. Risk of communal/caste polarisation when identity-based groups
dominate the discourse.
7. Urban-centric bias of many professional/associational groups, with
limited rural or informal-sector outreach.
8. Absence of institutional permanence for social-movement-type
(anomic) groups once the triggering issue fades.
Key comparative lesson for India
Mature democracies (US, UK, EU) have moved toward
statutory lobbying registers and disclosure requirements,
converting an informal/opaque practice into a
transparent, regulated one. India currently lacks any
equivalent central lobbying law — pressure group activity
remains governed indirectly through registration statutes
(Societies Registration Act, Companies Act) and funding
statutes (FCRA), rather than a dedicated lobbying
transparency framework.
Reform Recommendations / Way Forward
1. Enact a lobbying transparency law on the lines of the US Lobbying Disclosure Act or the EU Transparency Register, mandating
registration and disclosure of lobbying expenditure and meetings with public officials.
2. Institutionalise consultation mechanisms — mandatory public comment periods and Regulatory Impact Assessments (RIAs) before
major policy/regulatory changes, as practiced in OECD countries.
3. Rationalise FCRA implementation — balance transparency requirements with operational flexibility for genuine grassroots NGOs,
avoiding blanket restriction of civil society space.
4. Formalise informal/non-associational groups where feasible, to bring caste/ethnic/kinship advocacy into peaceful, accountable, and
traceable channels rather than leaving them to informal or occasionally coercive mobilisation.
5. Strengthen internal democracy and depoliticisation of trade unions and associational bodies to reduce excessive party co-option.
6. Encourage MSME and informal-sector inclusion in business-group consultations (FICCI/CII/ASSOCHAM), which currently skew
toward large corporates.
7. Promote national-level coordination among fragmented farmer/labour groups to reduce the localisation and caste/class divides seen
in movements like the farm law protests.
8. Leverage digital platforms for transparent, traceable donations and stakeholder engagement, reducing both foreign-funding anxieties
and domestic opacity.
