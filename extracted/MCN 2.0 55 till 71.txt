With over 5.5 crore cases pending across the Supreme Court, High Courts and the subordinate judiciary, delay has become the single
largest structural threat to the credibility of India's justice-delivery system — and to the constitutional promise of Article 21.
Intro
Source: National Judicial Data Grid (NJDG); PRS Legislative Research, Vital Stats — Pendency and Vacancies in the Judiciary
Composition and Nature of the Backlog
● Civil–criminal split: At the subordinate court level, roughly 77% of pending cases are criminal and 23% civil (March 2026 NJDG
data) — the reverse of the Supreme Court, where about 79% of pending matters are civil and 21% criminal.
● Government as litigant: The State (Union and State governments combined) is estimated to be a party in about 50% of all
pending cases — making the government itself the single largest litigant burdening the courts.
● Land and property disputes: These account for roughly 20% of all pending cases nationally and about 66% of pending civil
cases — also comprising about a quarter of matters decided by the Supreme Court.
● Age of pendency: Growth in pendency between 2010 and 2020 averaged about 2.8% a year; at prevailing disposal rates,
clearing the existing backlog (assuming no new filings) would take roughly 1.3 years for the Supreme Court and about 3 years
each for the High Courts and subordinate courts — an unrealistic counterfactual given that filings continue unabated.
● Undertrial prisoners: A large share of India's prison population consists of undertrials awaiting the conclusion of trial — many
for periods exceeding the maximum sentence prescribed for the alleged offence, a recurring concern flagged by the Supreme
Court and the NCRB's Prison Statistics reports.
India's judge-to-population ratio stood at roughly 21 judges per million population as of 2022 — far below the 50-per-million benchmark
recommended by the Law Commission of India and the Justice V.S. Malimath Committee

Structural and Systemic Causes
Higher Judiciary (Supreme Court and High Courts)
● Constitution Bench backlog: Complex constitutional questions
requiring larger (5-, 7- or 9-judge) benches often wait years for listing,
since regular benches are consumed by routine special leave petitions
(SLPs). This has a cascading effect — subordinate courts and High
Courts often stay proceedings pending an authoritative
pronouncement from the apex court.
● Litigation explosion at the Supreme Court via SLPs: Article 136
special leave jurisdiction has expanded far beyond its constitutional
design as an exceptional remedy, turning the Supreme Court into a
routine court of appeal and diluting its role as a constitutional court.
● Chronic High Court vacancies: Delays in the collegium–government
appointment process, including protracted file movement between
the collegium, state governments and the Union government, leave
large numbers of sanctioned posts unfilled for years at a time.
● Adjournment culture: Weak enforcement of the limits on
adjournments under the Code of Civil Procedure and want of firm
case-management by benches contribute to matters dragging on.
● Fragmented case law and forum-shopping: Divergent rulings by
different High Courts and benches on similar questions encourage
repeated litigation and appeals, adding to volumes.
Lower Judiciary (District and Subordinate Courts)
● Concentration of the crisis: Over 85% of all pending cases in the
country sit in the district and subordinate courts — the level at which
the ordinary citizen interacts most directly with the justice system —
making this the true epicentre of the pendency problem.
● Infrastructure deficit: Many district courts function with inadequate
courtroom space, understaffed registries, poor digitisation, and
shortages of court managers, translators and forensic support.
● Investigation and prosecution delays: Chargesheets filed late, poor
quality of investigation, forensic backlogs, and witness turning hostile
due to long delays all prolong criminal trials.
● Service of summons and appearance-stage delay: A very large share
of pending cases in subordinate courts (over 1.8 crore, per the
Supreme Court's own 'State of the Judiciary' report) are stuck merely
at the stage of appearance or service of summons — a problem of
process, not adjudication.
● Frequent adjournments and multiplicity of proceedings: Parallel civil
and criminal proceedings on the same set of facts, and routine grant
of adjournments, multiply hearings without disposing of matters.
● Government litigation: The State's own departments — being party
to roughly half of all pending cases — frequently fail to take timely
decisions on settlement, appeals or withdrawal of matters, keeping
cases alive unnecessarily.

Way Forward:
Judicial Strength and Appointments
1. Fill vacancies on a fixed timeline: Institutionalise a binding timeline (e.g., 3–6 months) between collegium recommendation and government
notification, potentially through a revised Memorandum of Procedure, to end the chronic HC/SC vacancy problem.
2. Rationalise judge strength to population and caseload: Move systematically toward the Law Commission's benchmark of 50 judges per
million population, calibrated to each state's actual case-filing trends rather than a uniform national formula.
3. Revive the All-India Judicial Service (AIJS): A centralised, merit-based recruitment mechanism for the subordinate judiciary (on the lines of
the civil services) can address regional disparities in quality and speed of recruitment, subject to safeguards for state autonomy and reservation
policy.
4. Strengthen the district judiciary cadre: Fast-track promotion pipelines and better service conditions to make district judgeships more
attractive and reduce attrition.
Court and Case Management Reforms
5. Differentiated case-flow management: Classify cases at filing into fast-track, standard and complex categories (as in Australia) with distinct
procedural timelines, rather than treating all matters identically.
6. Curb routine adjournments: Strict enforcement of the three-adjournment limit already provided in the Code of Civil Procedure, with costs
imposed for non-compliance.
7. Special benches for legacy cases: Extend the Supreme Court's 2026 model of dedicated benches for the oldest pending matters to High
Courts and district courts, with time-bound clearance targets.
8. Restrain expansive use of Article 136 SLPs: Encourage the Supreme Court to exercise stronger judicial discipline in admitting special leave
petitions, reserving its docket for matters of genuine constitutional or public importance, and refer routine appellate work to strengthened
High Courts.
9. National uniform case-management rules: A model set of case-management rules for civil and criminal trial courts, adopted uniformly across
states, to reduce procedural divergence.
Alternative Dispute Resolution and Negotiated Justice
10. Mainstream plea bargaining: Build institutional capacity — trained prosecutors, clear protocols, judicial oversight — so plea bargaining
moves beyond its current sub-1% usage toward the 15–30% range seen in continental Europe, with safeguards against coercion.
11. Expand Lok Adalats and mediation: Scale up National Lok Adalat drives and pre-litigation mediation (especially for matrimonial, motor-
accident-claims, cheque-bounce and commercial disputes), building on their demonstrated success.
12. Mandatory pre-litigation mediation: Extend the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 model of mandatory pre-institution mediation to a wider set
of civil and family disputes.
13. Reform lawyer remuneration structures: Align incentives so that lawyers and prosecutors have a professional interest in reaching a fair
settlement rather than prolonging litigation for billable appearances.
Technology and Infrastructure
14. Complete e-Courts Phase III on schedule: Ensure the ₹7,210 crore, four-year e-Courts Phase III programme (2023–2027) delivers fully
digital, paperless courts, expanded virtual-court capacity, and nationwide e-filing/e-payment adoption.
15. Responsible AI adoption: Use AI for case allocation, cause-list optimisation, translation, and legal research — as the Supreme Court's
reconstituted AI Committee is attempting — while heeding the CJI's caution that technology must remain 'a servant of justice, not its
substitute', particularly for litigants unfamiliar with digital tools.
16. Fix the appearance/service bottleneck: Since over 1.8 crore subordinate-court cases are stuck merely at the summons/appearance stage,
prioritise electronic and tracked service of summons, and empower process-serving agencies with better resources.
17. Invest in physical infrastructure: Sustain and scale up the Centrally Sponsored Scheme for district-court infrastructure (₹810 crore in
2026–27) to address courtroom shortages, especially in smaller towns.
Reducing Government Litigation
18. Implement the National Litigation Policy in letter and spirit: Empower government departments to take timely calls on withdrawing
frivolous appeals, and institute internal accountability for delay in litigation decisions, given the State's role as the single largest litigant.
19. Litigation audits: Periodic departmental audits of pending government litigation to identify cases fit for withdrawal or settlement.
Criminal Justice and Undertrial Reforms
20. Strengthen investigation quality: Better forensic infrastructure, trained investigating officers, and time-bound chargesheet filing to prevent
trial delays rooted in poor investigation.
21. Undertrial review committees: Ensure District Legal Services Authorities and undertrial review committees function actively to secure bail
or release for eligible undertrials, per Section 479 BNSS (equivalent to the erstwhile Section 436A CrPC).
22. Expand and sustain Fast Track Special Courts: Continue and expand the FTSC/ePOCSO scheme (775 courts currently) beyond its
September 2026 extension, with a permanent, adequately funded structure rather than repeated short-term renewals.
Institutional and Constitutional Measures
23. National Judicial Infrastructure Authority: Operationalise a permanent, statutorily backed body (as recommended in various policy
discussions) to plan, fund and monitor court infrastructure nationally, reducing dependence on ad hoc state-wise allocations.
24. Data-driven monitoring: Use the NJDG not merely as a public dashboard but as a management tool — with High Court Chief Justices and
District Judges held accountable for movement on pendency-reduction targets in their jurisdictions.
Legislative correction of appeal-heavy laws: Review procedural and substantive laws that generate excessive appellate litigation (e.g., taxation,
land acquisition) to build in finality at earlier stages

While the SC has been prompt to recognise Independence of Judiciary as Basic Structure in SC AoR Assn. v. Union of India (1994),
Judicial Accountability remains a delicate balance between Parliamentary Democracy and Public Trust.
8630 complaints against judges between 2016-2025 as per Law Minister’s reply to a Parliamentary Question.
Components of Judicial Independence
A. Structural/Institutional Independence
1.Separation of Powers: Judges are not part of legislature/executive (Article 50).
2.Security of Tenure: Judges of the SC/HC cannot be removed except by impeachment under Articles 124(4) and 217.
3.Financial Autonomy: Salaries charged on the Consolidated Fund; cannot be varied to a judge’s disadvantage (Article 125, 221).
4.Appointment process: Collegium system gives primacy to the judiciary in appointments and transfers (Second and Third Judges cases).
5.Fixed Service Conditions: Articles 125 and 221 ensure that the salaries and allowances of Supreme Court and High Court judges are fixed
and cannot be varied to their disadvantage during their term
B. Functional Independence
1.No Interference in Judicial Functions: Courts cannot be directed on how to decide cases (Article 141).
2.Control over Court Proceedings: Judges control procedure, contempt powers, listing, etc.
3.Prohibition on Discussion of Judicial Conduct: Articles 121 and 211 prohibit the discussion of a judge’s conduct in Parliament or State
Legislatures except in the context of impeachment, protecting judges from political pressure. Supplemented by Judges Enquiry Act, 1968
4.Powers and Jurisdiction of Supreme Court: Parliament can only add to the powers and jurisdiction of the Supreme Court but cannot
curtail them
5.Power to punish for contempt: Both the Supreme Court and the High Court have the power to punish any person for their contempt. (Art
129/215)
C. Internal Independence
1.Freedom from influence within the judiciary (e.g., pressure from superiors like Chief Justices).
D. Decisional Independence
1.Judges decide cases "without fear or favour, affection or ill-will" (Third Schedule – Oath of Office)

Components of Judicial Accountability
A. Constitutional Accountability
1.Impeachment Process: Judges can be removed for "proved misbehaviour or incapacity" under Articles 124(4) and 217(1)(b).
2.Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968: Outlines the detailed procedure for impeachment, requiring a motion by at least 100 Lok Sabha or 50 Rajya
Sabha members, investigation by a three-member committee, and approval by a two-thirds majority in both Houses of Parliament.
3.Declaration of Assets: Supreme Court Resolution (1997), though not binding.
4.High Court Control Over Subordinate Judiciary: Art.235 gives High Courts control over the subordinate judiciary, ensuring
accountability at lower level
B. Institutional Accountability
1.In-House Procedure: Established by SC for internal inquiry into complaints.
a.K. Veeraswami v. Union of India (1991) - Prior CJI permission needed to investigate judges
2.Restatements of Judicial Values (1999): Ethical code adopted by the judiciary.
C. Public Accountability
1.Reasoned Judgments: Judgments are public documents and subject to appeal and academic scrutiny.
a.CPIO, SC v Subhash Chandra Agarwal (2010) - case by case basis
2.Contempt of Court: While meant to protect dignity, excessive use can stifle legitimate criticism.
E. Case Studies:
Justice V. Ramaswami: Impeachment failed in 1993 due to political abstentions
Justice Soumitra Sen: Resigned before impeachment could be completed, Justice Dinakaran: Resigned on the day of the inquiry
Justice Varma (Del HC) currently in news, J. Gogoi (Conflict of Interest)
Key Issues
High Threshold for Removal: The Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968, and Articles 124(4), 217(1)(b), and 218 of the Constitution require a
motion signed by at least 100 Lok Sabha or 50 Rajya Sabha members, investigation by a three-member committee, and a two-thirds
majority in both Houses of Parliament to remove a judge for “proven misbehaviour or incapacity”. This makes removal extremely rare
and difficult.
Political Influence and Delay: The process is susceptible to political machinations and can be delayed or derailed by partisan interests.
Lack of Intermediate Sanctions: The law provides no mechanism for censure, suspension, or lesser penalties short of removal, making
the process all-or-nothing.
In-House Mechanism Limitations: The SC ’s in-house procedure for complaints lacks statutory backing and transparency, and
decisions are not publicly disclosed
Resignation as a Loophole: Judges can resign to avoid impeachment, halting investigations and escaping accountability.
Limited Transparency: There is no mandatory public disclosure of judicial assets or liabilities, and the impeachment process lacks clear
timelines and public accountability.
Judicial independence is not a privilege of judges—it is the right of every citizen to an impartial arbiter. The moment courts bend to power or
prejudice, justice becomes a casualty and democracy, its silent victim.
Judicial accountability is not an attack on independence—it's its anchor. In a democracy, where judges wield immense power, silence on their
conduct is not neutrality, but negligence. Justice must not only be done, but must be seen to be answerable.

Providing Security of Tenure and Fixed Salaries (Articles 124, 125, 217, 221), ensuring independence.

Limitations of Judicial Review
1.Constitutional Immunities:
a.Presidential Immunity: Under Article 361, the President and Governors enjoy immunity from judicial proceedings for
acts done in their official capacity. This limits the courts' ability to review certain executive actions.
b.Judicial Immunity: Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts are protected from legal action for their judicial
conduct, as per Article 124(4) and 218.
c.Ninth Schedule: Laws placed in the Ninth Schedule of the Constitution were initially immune from judicial review.
However, the Supreme Court in I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007) held that laws added to the Ninth Schedule
after April 24, 1973, are subject to judicial review if they violate fundamental rights or the basic structure of the
Constitution.
2.Separation of Powers: The doctrine of separation of powers imposes inherent limitations on judicial review:
a.Political Questions: Courts generally refrain from interfering in matters considered "political questions" that fall within
the purview of the legislature or executive.
b.Policy Decisions: The judiciary avoids reviewing the wisdom or correctness of policy decisions made by the executive,
as long as they are within constitutional bounds.
3.Procedural and Practical Constraints:
a.Locus Standi: Only parties with sufficient interest in a case can approach the courts for judicial review, limiting
frivolous litigation.
b.Time Limitations: There are often time limits within which judicial review can be sought, preventing indefinite
challenges to government actions.
c.Res Judicata: The principle of res judicata prevents re-litigation of issues already decided by courts, limiting repeated
challenges on the same grounds.
4.Judicial Self-Restraint: The judiciary often exercises self-restraint to avoid overreach (Supriyo Case, Same Sex Marriages)
5.Deference to Expertise: Courts often defer to the expertise of specialised bodies or administrative authorities in technical
matters.
6.Presumption of Constitutionality: Laws enacted by the legislature are presumed to be constitutional, and the burden of
proving otherwise lies on the challenger.

Art 142 : Complete Justice
Rationale
1.Grant relief to do “complete
justice” in a given case beyond
the applicable statutory provisions
2.issue directions to fill, what the
court perceives as “legislative
gaps”, which directions operate as
the law of the land until such time
that the legislature or the
executive steps in.
3.Do not hold precedent value
Analysis
1.Pros:
a.Equitable Justice: Resolves unique cases (e.g., Union Carbide
Compensation, 1989, for Bhopal victims).
b.Systemic Reforms: Enables structural changes (e.g., Lakshmi Kant Pandey
v. UOI, 1984, regulating inter-country adoptions).
c.Ensures Complete Justice : Perarivalan Case (2022)
d.Protects Public Interest: In Savelife Foundation v. UOI (2016
2.Cons:
a.Unchecked Authority: Risks judicial overreach (e.g., Reliance Natural
Resources Ltd. v. Reliance Industries, 2010, rewriting contracts).
b.Legal Uncertainty: Erodes statutory predictability (e.g., Coal Block
Allocation cancellations, 2014, disrupting industries).
“Paradoxically, the greatest danger to the administration of justice and constitutional interpretation arises from the
genuine desire of Judges to do justice in each individual case.”... HM Seervai
Research published by IIM-Ahmedabad in May 2024 suggests that of the 1579 Supreme Court cases where the terms
“Article 142” or “complete justice” were used, instances of ambiguous usage in almost 40% of cases
In Supreme Court Bar Association v Union of India (1998), SC referred to the provision as “a valuable weapon in the hands
of the Court to prevent clogging or obstruction of the stream of injustice.” But it also imposed a kind of fetter on the
provision by noting that it can “supplement” but not “supplant” the substantive law under consideration.
For a direction under Article 142 to be effective, there needs to be an implementation mechanism. [Pravasi Bhalai
Sangathan v Union of India (2014)]
Intro
As long as there are intersections between law and public life, there will be a place for the Court’s discretionary power under Article
142. And as long as the rule of law prevails, there will be calls to use it sparingly, at each time upholding constitutional principles and
respecting the separation of powers

Judicial Activism / Positive Rights Expansion
• Bulldozer justice curbs (Demolition of Structures, 2024-25) - judiciary checking executive excess and
protecting the right to shelter through pan-India binding guidelines and compensation orders.
• Chambal sand mining & Aravalli suo motu interventions - judiciary filling a coordination vacuum
among States on ecologically sensitive matters, using continuing mandamus and Article 142.
• Human trafficking victims' right to rehabilitation (Prajwala case) - Court fashions a comprehensive
'Victim Protection Plan' in the continued absence of a dedicated central law (since 2004).
• Passive euthanasia extension to CANH (Harish Rana case) - incremental but significant extension of
the Common Cause (2018) framework to a new category of medical treatment.
• Right to safe highways / footpaths - novel extensions of Article 21/19(1)(d) responding to road-safety
data, using Article 142 to issue nationwide operational directions.
B. Judicial Restraint / Self-Correction
• CREDAI v. Vanashakti (recall of the ex post facto EC ban) - a rare instance of the Court reviewing and

strong dissent warning against retrogression from the precautionary principle).
• Waqf (Amendment) Act interim order - Court declined a blanket stay of a parliamentary statute,
staying only specific provisions, reaffirming that interim relief against legislation should be exceptional.
• Bihar SIR judgment (ADR v. ECI) - deference to the Election Commission's plenary power under Article
324, even while using earlier interim orders to protect voters from arbitrary exclusion.
• Aravalli definition flip-flop - the Court's willingness to stay its own November 2025 order after
recognising the earlier expert committee's recommendations may have been 'misconstrued', and to
reconstitute a fresh committee.
C. Contested / Criticised Instances of Activism
• NCERT Class 8 textbook suo motu ban - criticised as the judiciary acting as 'protector and prosecutor'
of its own institutional reputation without full adversarial hearing; raises due-process concerns even
where the underlying discomfort (an unapproved chapter naming judicial corruption) is understandable.
• Menstrual leave PIL - Court's refusal to mandate a uniform paid-leave policy (fearing hiring bias)
shows a self-aware limit on how far rights-language can be translated into binding economic mandates,

D. Doctrinal Anchors
• Article 142 ('complete justice'): used in All India Judges Assn. (service law), Phalodi Accident (road
safety), and the Chambal sand-mining proceedings - but its limits were reaffirmed in the Allahabad HC
Bar Association line of reasoning: Article 142 cannot override statute, nullify accrued third-party rights,
or bypass natural justice.
• Suo motu cognisance: exercised in NCERT (institutional dignity), Chambal (ecology), and stray-animal
(public safety) matters - useful to discuss both its emergency-response value (COVID migrant crisis
precedent) and its risks (selective justice, absence of a formal petitioner/adversarial process).
• Continuing mandamus: visible in the Demolition of Structures, Chambal sand-mining, and stray-dog
proceedings - the Court retains seisin over a matter through repeated compliance hearings rather than a
one-time order.
• Judicial review of legislation: Waqf Amendment Act case is the clearest 2025-26 example of the
'presumption of constitutionality' standard being applied to limit interim stays of parliamentary
enactments.

Rationale Behind the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996
1.Minimise Judicial Intervention: The Act sought to limit the supervisory role of courts in arbitral proceedings, aiming to
expedite the resolution of disputes and reduce the burden on the judicial system.
2.Promote Efficiency and Finality: The Act was designed to make arbitral procedures fair, efficient, and capable of meeting
the needs of specific arbitrations, ensuring that arbitral awards are final, binding, and enforceable as a decree of the court.
3.Align with International Standards: The 1996 Act was based on the United Nations Commission on International Trade
Law (UNCITRAL) Model Law on International Commercial Arbitration, bringing Indian law into conformity with global best
practices and making India an arbitration-friendly jurisdiction.
4.Encourage Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR): The Act was part of a broader movement to promote ADR mechanisms,
such as arbitration, conciliation, and mediation, as alternatives to traditional litigation, which is often slow, expensive, and
procedurally rigid.
5.Support Economic Liberalisation: The Act was introduced in the context of India’s economic liberalization in the 1990s,
aiming to attract foreign investment by providing a reliable and efficient dispute resolution mechanis

a.Timelines: Introduced strict timelines for the completion of arbitral proceedings (12 months, extendable by 6 months).

Civil jurisdiction includes property disputes, labor disputes, and other
matters; criminal jurisdiction covers offenses punishable by up to two

Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (now the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam),
but are guided by the principles of natural justice and any rules made
by the High Court.
5.Conciliation: The Act mandates that civil disputes should first be
attempted to be resolved through conciliation before proceeding to
trial.
6.Appeals: Appeals in criminal cases lie to the Court of Session and in
civil cases to the District Court, with a strict six-month timeline for
disposal.

Why the Judiciary Cannot Ignore AI
● Access to justice: Backlogs, cost of litigation, and language
barriers keep large sections of citizens — especially the poor
and rural population — outside the practical reach of courts.
● Capacity constraints: Persistent vacancies (roughly a fifth to
a quarter of sanctioned judicial posts lie vacant at various

● Administrative overload: Judges spend disproportionate
time on scheduling, record-keeping and research — tasks that
do not require judicial mind application but consume judicial
bandwidth.
● Global competitiveness: Ease of doing business and
contract enforcement rankings are tied to how quickly
commercial disputes are resolved; AI-enabled efficiency has a
direct economic dividend.
● Irreversibility of early choices: The institutional architecture
and standard-setting mechanisms decided now will be very
hard to reverse once AI is embedded in courts — making the

Contested Issues
● Conflict of interest: the Apex Body that regulates AI in courts is itself appointed predominantly by sitting judges of the
same judiciary it is meant to oversee — raising concerns about independence of standard-setting.
● Fragmented standards: because the Draft Regulations do not define uniform, mandatory technical benchmarks (acceptable
error rates, explainability thresholds, training-data provenance), each of India’s 25 High Courts may set different approval
standards, risking inconsistent adoption and, in effect, inconsistent justice.
● Disclosure burden: the Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association (SCAORA) has opposed the blanket AI-disclosure
requirement for lawyers (Regulation 43) as unworkable given existing professional-conduct norms.
● Capacity gap: in-house audit requirements and a bar on sharing source code with external parties presuppose a level of
technical capacity that most courts do not yet have.
● Interface with the broader national AI-governance framework (e.g., the Ministry of Electronics and IT’s AI governance
guidelines) remains undefined.
Challenges and Concerns
Structural and Institutional
● Fragmented governance: successive waves of court technology (computerisation, digitisation, video-conferencing, AI) have
each spawned separate committees, fragmenting accountability and slowing decision-making instead of consolidating
expertise in one durable institution.
● No permanent execution body: unlike the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI) for UPI or the Unique
Identification Authority of India (UIDAI) for Aadhaar, the judiciary lacks a permanent, well-resourced institution with the
ownership and continuity needed to execute a technology transformation of this scale.
Standards and Accountability
● Absence of uniform technical standards: what a system must explain, to whom, and what error rate is tolerable in a bail
matter versus a scheduling task remain undefined, leaving each High Court to set its own bar.
● Standard-setter and procurer overlap: unless a neutral body such as CoRE-AI is genuinely free of procurement interest,
standards risk being set by the same body that benefits from a particular vendor or system succeeding.
● Explainability and the ‘black box’ problem: many advanced AI/ML models cannot fully explain their own outputs, in tension
with due process requirements that litigants understand and contest the basis of decisions affecting them.
Bias, Fairness and Rights
● Algorithmic bias: predictive tools trained on historical data can encode and amplify existing social biases (caste, religion,
gender, economic status) — a risk starkly illustrated abroad by the COMPAS recidivism-scoring controversy in the United
States, where risk scores were shown to correlate with socio-economic factors rather than culpability.
● Right to a fair trial and natural justice: outsourcing any part of evidentiary evaluation, bail assessment or sentencing logic to
opaque algorithms risks diluting audi alteram partem and reasoned adjudication.
● Access and the digital divide: AI-driven efficiency for e-filing and virtual hearings can inadvertently disadvantage litigants
without digital literacy, devices or connectivity — particularly in rural India.
Data Privacy and Security
● Judicial data includes highly sensitive personal, medical and financial information; AI training and deployment raise
questions of data ownership, retention, vendor access and cross-border data flows.
● Interface with the DPDPA, 2023 is not yet fully settled for court-generated and litigant data.
● Cybersecurity risk grows with every additional AI-enabled touchpoint integrated into ICMIS/CIS.
Human and Institutional Capacity
● Judges, court staff and even lawyers vary widely in digital literacy across India’s 25 High Courts and thousands of
subordinate courts, risking uneven and inequitable adoption.
● Vendor dependency: private technology providers may claim IP over tools built substantially on judicial data, creating long-
term lock-in risks if contractual safeguards are weak.
● Overburdened existing bodies: the e-Committee and AI Committee, with rotating memberships, may lack the permanence
and dedicated staffing needed for sustained oversight.

Global Best Practices and Comparative Experience
Recommendations
Institutional Reform
1. Consolidate the fragmented committee architecture into a single, permanent, adequately staffed institution — modelled on
UIDAI/NPCI’s institutional permanence.
2. Ensure CoRE-AI’s genuine neutrality by insulating it from procurement decisions and vendor relationships, consistent with the
UPI-style separation of standard-setter from market participant.
3. Address the conflict-of-interest critique by including a meaningful proportion of independent technologists, civil-society and bar
representatives in the Apex Body, beyond judicial nominees.
Standards and Technical Safeguards
4. Notify uniform, mandatory technical standards — acceptable error rates by use-case, explainability requirements, and training-
data provenance disclosures — so that approvals do not vary arbitrarily across 25 High Courts.
5. Mandate algorithmic audits and bias-testing (including disaggregated testing across caste, gender, religion and region) before any
AI tool is approved for court use, with published audit summaries.
6. Retain and strengthen the non-derogable prohibitions on bail, recidivism, witness-credibility and black-box liberty-affecting uses
— these should not be diluted through future amendment.
Access, Capacity and Inclusion
7. Scale AI-enabled multilingual and voice-interface tools (SUVAS, PANINI, ASR-SHRUTI) to district and taluka courts, not just the
Supreme Court and High Courts, to close the urban-rural access gap.
8. Invest in digital literacy training for judges, court staff, and legal-aid lawyers, paired with continued physical/offline access (e-
Seva Kendras, District Legal Services Authorities) so digitisation does not become a new barrier for the digitally excluded.
9. Extend AI-assisted guidance tools (on the Singapore Small Claims Tribunal model) to Lok Adalats, Gram Nyayalayas and consumer
fora to help unrepresented litigants.
Data Protection and Cybersecurity
10.Specific judicial-data protection protocol given the special sensitivity of case data.
11.Contractually bar vendors from claiming IP over models substantially trained on judicial data, and mandate data-localisation and
access-logging for any AI system handling case records.
Legislative and Constitutional Clarity
12.Consider placing the core non-derogable prohibitions and the Apex Body’s standard-setting mandate on a statutory footing (via
Parliamentary legislation or rules under Article 145), rather than relying solely on Supreme Court regulations, to give the framework
durability beyond any one Chief Justice’s tenure.
13.Build periodic parliamentary and public reporting (annual governance reports, as already proposed for the Apex Body) into the
framework to maintain democratic accountability over an unelected standard-setting body.
Phased, Evidence-Based Rollout
14.Continue current practice of piloting tools (e.g., e-filing defect scrutiny, SUPACE) before full deployment, with transparent
evaluation criteria and sunset/review clauses.
15.Institutionalise feedback loops from litigants, the Bar and civil society (as being done through the current public-comment
process) into a recurring, not one-time, consultation cycle as AI capability evolves.

Government and the Supreme Court in describing AI’s role in India’s courts
