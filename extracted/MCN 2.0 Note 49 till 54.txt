Mains Concise Note (MCN) 49 : President’s Role in a Parl Democracy
The President of India, as the constitutional Head of State under a parliamentary system, occupies a position that is at once symbolic and
structurally indispensable
Constitutional and Functional Dimensions of the President's Role
1. Nominal Executive Head (Articles 52–78)
All executive action of the Union is formally taken in the name of the
President (Article 77), even though it is the Council of Ministers that decides.
Appoints the Prime Minister, other Ministers, Governors, the CAG, Election
Commissioners, judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts, and members
of UPSC.
Bound by the "aid and advice" of the Council of Ministers under Article
74(1); post the 42nd and 44th Amendments, this advice is binding, though
the President may require reconsideration once.
2. Legislative Role
Summons, prorogues, and can dissolve the Lok Sabha; addresses the first
session after each general election and the first session of each year (Article
87).
Assent to bills (Article 111): the President may give assent, withhold assent,
or return a bill (except a Money Bill) for reconsideration.
No fixed timeline exists for assent, giving rise to the phenomenon of the
"pocket veto" — indefinite inaction, which is neither assent nor refusal.
Certain state bills reserved by the Governor under Article 200 require
Presidential assent, and rejection of the bills leads of the death of the bill.
3. Ordinance-Making Power (Article 123)
Enables the Union Executive to legislate when Parliament is not in session,
subject to Cabinet advice and subsequent parliamentary approval within six
weeks of reassembly.
Originally an emergency provision borrowed from the Government of India
Act, 1935; India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh remain rare examples of
parliamentary democracies retaining ordinance-making power at this scale.
4. Judicial Power — Pardoning (Article 72)
Power to grant pardon, reprieve, respite, remission, or commutation of
sentence, including in death sentence cases — broader in scope than the
Governor's parallel power under Article 161.
Exercised on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (Home Ministry),
yet subject to limited judicial review for arbitrariness, mala fide exercise, or
unexplained delay.
5. Financial Role
Money Bills can only be introduced in Parliament with prior Presidential
recommendation (Article 117); the Annual Financial Statement (Budget) is
laid before Parliament in the President's name (Article 112); Contingency
Fund of India is at the President's disposal (Article 267).
6. Diplomatic and Military Role
Represents India in international affairs; treaties and international
agreements are negotiated and ratified in the President's name, subject to
Parliament's role.
Supreme Commander of the Defence Forces (Article 53(2)) — a formal, non-
operational role; actual military decision-making rests with the Cabinet
Committee on Security.
7. Emergency Powers (Articles 352, 356, 360)
National Emergency (external aggression/armed rebellion), President's Rule
in states (failure of constitutional machinery), and Financial Emergency.
These represent the most consequential — and most contested — dimension
of the office, since they permit the President (in effect, the Union Executive)
to override the federal division of power.
8. Discretionary/Reserve Powers
Though ordinarily bound by ministerial advice, situations exist where the
President exercises independent judgment:
Appointing a Prime Minister in a hung Lok Sabha, or when the incumbent
PM dies/resigns suddenly without a clear successor.
Dismissing a government that has demonstrably lost majority support but
refuses to resign.
Returning advice for reconsideration once under the proviso to Article 74(1).
Deciding whether to act on advice of a Council of Ministers that has lost
confidence but continues in office in a caretaker capacity.
Issues in Fulfilling the Presidential Role
1. Ambiguity of "Aid and Advice" and Reserve Powers
The Constitution does not exhaustively define situations of genuine
discretion, leaving the President to rely on convention rather than codified
rule — a vacuum that becomes acute in coalition politics and hung verdicts.
2. The Pocket Veto Problem
Unlike the U.S. President, whose pocket veto is time-bound (10 days), the
Indian President faces no deadline on withholding assent, enabling bills to
remain in indefinite limbo — most famously, the Postal Bill of 1986, never
signed by President Zail Singh and left to lapse.
3. Politicisation of Discretionary Choices in Hung Verdicts
Decisions on who is invited to form the government in fractured mandates
(1989, 1996, 1998, 2004 at the Centre; frequent at state level) have
repeatedly drawn charges of partisan bias, since no codified "post-poll
alliance" protocol exists.
4. Delay and Inaction on Bills and Mercy Petitions
Mercy petitions under Article 72 have historically taken years — even
decades — to decide, raising Article 21 concerns about "death row limbo."
Similarly, reserved state bills have lain pending for years without a
Presidential decision, prompting the Supreme Court's 2025 intervention in
State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor to impose timelines (Governor: 1–3
months; President: 3 months on Governor-referred bills).
5. Ordinance Overuse ("Ordinance Raj")
Though meant to be exceptional, ordinance re-promulgation has been
frequent, especially in the 1970s–1990s and at the state level, effectively
bypassing legislative scrutiny (e.g., the Delhi Services Ordinance, 2023, and
repeated Commercial Courts ordinances).
6. Misuse of Emergency Provisions
Article 356 (President's Rule) has been invoked close to 130 times since
1950, frequently for partisan reasons rather than genuine constitutional
breakdown — a practice later curbed, though not eliminated, by S.R.
Bommai v. Union of India (1994).
7. Erosion of Neutrality and Perception of Executive Capture
Because the President is elected by an Electoral College dominated by the
ruling coalition and often has a prior political career, questions of
independence recur — particularly regarding assent to contentious bills
(e.g., farm laws, CAA) or silence on matters of institutional friction.
8. Weak Accountability Mechanisms
Impeachment under Article 61 is constitutionally so onerous (special
majority in both Houses, charge investigation) that it has never been used,
leaving few real checks on the manner (as opposed to the fact) of exercise
of Presidential functions.
9. Governor–President Interface Tensions
Politically charged Governors reserving bills for President's consideration as
a delaying tactic (Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Punjab, West Bengal in recent years)
transfers federal friction upward to the Presidential office, entangling it in
Centre–state disputes it was designed to stand above.
10. Absence of a Real Check on Prime Ministerial Advice
Since the 44th Amendment made the President's ability to seek
reconsideration a one-time, non-binding option, the office has effectively
no substantive veto over Cabinet decisions — a design choice that
preserves parliamentary supremacy but limits the President's capacity to
act as a genuine constitutional brake.

Case Studies
Zail Singh and the Postal Bill (1986): President Giani Zail Singh neither signed nor rejected the Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, effectively using a
"pocket veto" — illustrating the constitutional gap around assent timelines that persisted until the 2025 Supreme Court intervention.
K.R. Narayanan and Bihar President's Rule (1998): President Narayanan returned the Union Cabinet's recommendation to impose President's Rule in
Bihar for reconsideration — a rare, assertive use of the Article 74(1) proviso, seen as a landmark moment of Presidential independence.
A.P.J. Abdul Kalam and the Office of Profit Bill (2006): President Kalam initially withheld assent and sought reconsideration of the Office of Profit
(Removal of Disqualification) Bill; when returned by Parliament unchanged, he was constitutionally bound to sign it — illustrating the limits of the
President's suspensive veto.
S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994): Though concerning Governors and Article 356, the judgment squarely constrained the President's emergency
powers, mandating floor tests, judicial reviewability of "satisfaction," and restrictions on assembly dissolution before parliamentary approval.
State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor R.N. Ravi (2025): The Supreme Court, for the first time, fixed timelines binding both the Governor and the President for
bill assent, directly responding to years of legislative paralysis caused by indefinite reservation of bills — a case as much about Presidential accountability
as gubernatorial conduct. This was reversed via a Presidential Reference under Art 143
Droupadi Murmu's Presidency (2022– ): As India's first tribal woman President, her tenure has been read as both a symbolic milestone in representative
politics and a test case for how far ceremonial visibility translates into substantive institutional assertiveness.
Delhi Office of Profit Disqualifications (2018): President Ram Nath Kovind, acting on the Election Commission's recommendation, disqualified 20 Delhi
MLAs for holding "office of profit" as Parliamentary Secretaries — an example of the President exercising a quasi-judicial constitutional function based
on independent constitutional body advice, later stayed by the Delhi High Court.
Way Forward / Reform Solutions
Codify Timelines for Assent: Extend the Supreme Court's 2025 directive into a constitutional or statutory amendment fixing outer limits for
Presidential decisions on bills, ordinances, and mercy petitions, eliminating the pocket veto ambiguity.
Develop a Post-Poll Protocol for Hung Verdicts: Following practices like Germany's and Israel's structured consultation processes, India could
evolve a transparent, publicly known convention (single largest party first, then pre-poll alliances, then post-poll coalitions with a floor test
within a fixed period) to reduce the perception of partisan discretion.
Limit and Guide Emergency Powers: Building on S.R. Bommai, further legislative guidelines (as recommended by the Sarkaria and Punchhi
Commissions) should govern the "satisfaction" standard for Article 356, mandating a reasoned, speaking order from the Governor before any
Presidential proclamation.
Introduce a Constitutional Reference Power: Consider an Ireland-style mechanism allowing the President to refer a bill to the Supreme Court
for an advisory opinion on constitutionality before assent, strengthening the office's role as a genuine, apolitical safeguard (distinct from the
existing Article 143 reference, which is government-initiated).
Institutionalise an Independent Clemency Review Body: To depoliticise Article 72 decisions and prevent prolonged death-row uncertainty, an
independent clemency board with fixed timelines and transparent criteria could support (not replace) Presidential decision-making.
Strengthen Transparency in Ordinance Use: Mandate public disclosure of the "urgency" justification for each ordinance and cap re-
promulgation, as recommended in various Law Commission reports, to prevent the ordinance route from substituting for regular legislative
process.
Insulate the Office from Political Career Pipelines: While not requiring a formal bar, evolving a stronger convention — akin to the Speaker's
post-election detachment from party affiliation in the UK — for Presidential candidates to visibly distance themselves from active partisan roles
would bolster perceived neutrality.
Empower Judicial Review of Delay, Not Just Decision: Extend the logic used for mercy petitions (unexplained delay as grounds for
commutation) to bill assent and other Presidential functions, ensuring that inaction itself becomes constitutionally reviewable.
The President of India was conceived not as a rubber stamp but as a constitutional sentinel — one whose authority lies less in the frequency of its
exercise and more in its availability as a check when the ordinary machinery of parliamentary accountability falters.

The office of the Vice-President of India embodies the Constitution’s vision of continuity, balance, and neutrality —
ensuring that neither the Executive nor the Parliament ever faces a vacuum in authority.
Intro
Rationale
1.Presiding Over Proceedings : Ensures orderly conduct, regulates debate, and maintains discipline.
i.2018 – VP Venkaiah Naidu rejected the impeachment motion against CJI Dipak Misra, ruling it as "lacking
merit". Though contested, it reflected the Chairman’s power to prevent politically motivated motions from
disrupting judicial-administrative stability.
ii.2023 – VP Jagdeep Dhankhar firmly intervened to prevent disruptions during discussion on the women’s
reservation bill, pressing members to maintain decorum.
2.Interpretation and Enforcement of Rules : Authority to interpret rules under Rule 266 of RS Rules
a.2015 – Hamid Ansari permitted calling attention motion by Opposition on rising intolerance despite government
objection. Interpreted rules to uphold democratic debate.
b.2023 – Dhankhar disallowed multiple short-duration discussions citing Rule 267, sparking contention with
Opposition, illustrating the Chairman's discretionary reach.
3.Deciding on Admissibility of Motions & Questions : Has powers to admit/deny adjournment, privilege, or censure
motions.
a.2012 – Hamid Ansari admitted FDI in retail discussion despite government’s hesitation, enabling debate on major
policy reform.
b.2023 – Dhankhar refused Opposition's motion to discuss Pegasus surveillance issue under Rule 267, triggering
debate on parliamentary accountability.
4.Casting Vote (In Case of Tie) : Similar to US VP’s Senate role, though rare due to party dominance in India.
a.1972 – VP Gopal Swarup Pathak cast a tie-breaking vote on the Payment of Bonus Bill, influencing labour welfare
legislation.
5.Committee-Related Administrative Powers : Nominates members to parliamentary committees.
a.2004 – Bhairon Singh Shekhawat ensured cross-party inclusion in the Ethics Committee during the cash-for-query
scandal, enhancing credibility.
6.Upholding Decorum & Discipline : Can suspend members for unruly conduct (Rule 256).
a.2021 – M. Venkaiah Naidu suspended 12 Opposition MPs during Winter Session for disruptions, though criticized
as excessive.
7.Ensuring Federal Balance : As head of the States’ House, the Chairman must facilitate federal dialogue.
a.During GST debate (2016), Hamid Ansari ensured regional concerns from Tamil Nadu and West Bengal were heard,
leading to compensation guarantees in GST framework.
8.Facilitating Debate Beyond Political Polarisation : Encourages deliberation focused on national interest.
a.Post 26/11 Mumbai attacks (2008), VP Hamid Ansari promoted collective debate on national security without
partisan blame, leading to consensus on revamping intelligence architecture.
9.Constitutional & Ethical Custodian : Expected to be impartial though often criticised for perceived bias.
a.2023 – VP Dhankhar publicly criticised Supreme Court’s remarks on basic structure doctrine, raising controversy
over neutrality expected from RS Chairman.

Impact of Cabinet Form of Government on Parliamentary Democracy
Strengths of the Cabinet System
Fusion of Powers = Responsive Government
• Unlike the US presidential system with its separation of powers, India's cabinet system fuses executive and legislative power.
• The PM and Cabinet must command legislative majority — this ensures executive-legislative alignment and stable policy passage.
Accountability Mechanism
• Question Hour, Zero Hour, No-Confidence Motions — all are accountability tools that work because ministers are also
parliamentarians.
• The executive cannot ignore the legislature as can happen in a presidential system.
Collective Decision-Making
• In theory, Cabinet government means collective wisdom. No single person can unilaterally commit the state — theoretically.
How the Cabinet System Harms Parliamentary Democracy
Party Whip and Anti-Defection: Legislator as Rubber Stamp
• The 10th Schedule and party whip system mean parliamentarians must vote with the party/government.
• A backbencher MP cannot effectively criticise the government in Parliament without risking disqualification.
• The Cabinet, which is supposed to be accountable to Parliament, effectively controls Parliament through its majority and whip.
Coalition Compulsions and Policy Paralysis
•Coalition CoMs must negotiate policy with partners — leading to policy paralysis (UPA 2: 2010–2014 was marked by decision-making
gridlock).
•No-action as policy: When coalition partners disagree, the safest option is inaction.
Question Hour Dysfunctionality
•PRS Legislative Research data: In recent years, Parliament meets for fewer days (average 70 days per year vs. 120+ days in Nehru era).
•Ministers use supplementary questions as an opportunity for political point-scoring, not substantive answers.
•Starred questions (oral answers) get answered for only a fraction of questions listed.
Delegated Legislation — Parliament's Powers Hollowed Out
•Ministers increasingly govern through Rules, Regulations, and Notifications under enabling legislation — without Parliamentary
oversight.
•Example: COVID-19 lockdown, demonetisation, abrogation of Art. 370 — all executed with minimal Parliament time, using
ministerial/executive powers.
The Fusion that Corrupts: Executive Control of Legislature
•The majority government effectively controls the Lok Sabha agenda through the Business Advisory Committee where the
government has numerical dominance.
•Bills increasingly passed without Committee scrutiny: PRS data shows between 2019–2023, many bills were passed without referring
to Standing Committees.
•This inverts constitutional logic: Cabinet should be accountable TO Parliament, but controls Parliament instead.

• UK: The Ministerial Code is a detailed document codifying ministers' ethical obligations, conflict of interest rules, use of

Criminalisation of the Council of Ministers
One of the gravest structural issues in Indian democracy is the presence of
ministers with serious criminal antecedents.
• ADR Report 2024: Of 543 Lok Sabha MPs elected in 2024, 251 (46%)
have declared criminal cases against them.
• In the Modi 3.0 Cabinet (2024): Several ministers have declared criminal
cases at the time of oath-taking.
• Mafia-to-Minister pipeline: Atiq Ahmed (Uttar Pradesh), D.P. Yadav —
examples of politicians with serious criminal records entering legislatures
and influencing ministerial appointments.
Oversized Cabinets (Pre-91st Amendment)
•Before 2003, there was no cap on cabinet size. Cabinets were expanded
for political reasons — to accommodate caste, regional, and coalition
compulsions.
•H.D. Deve Gowda's Cabinet (1996): 33 Cabinet Ministers + numerous
MoS — called 'the largest and most diverse Cabinet.'
•Indira Gandhi's cabinets at times exceeded 60 members when all grades
included.
91st Amendment (2003) introduced the 15% cap — a reform response to
this bloat.
Conflict of Interest and Portfolio Allocation
•Ministers often get portfolios in sectors where they have personal
business interests.
•No dedicated conflict of interest legislation for ministers in India (unlike
Australia, Canada, UK).
•Example: Mining industry insiders as Mines Ministers; real estate
developers as Housing Ministers.
Lack of Ministerial Expertise
•No constitutional requirement for any subject expertise for a ministerial
appointment.
•Health Ministry managed by those with no medical/public health
background; Finance Ministry by non-economists.
Contrast: France, Germany, Scandinavian countries frequently appoint
technocrats/experts.
Bureaucratic Capture
•Ministers are politically powerful but often dependent on senior IAS/IFS
officers for technical guidance.
•This can lead to 'file raj' — where bureaucrats set the real agenda and
ministers merely rubber-stamp.
•Or the reverse: politicisation of the bureaucracy where ministers
transfer/post officers based on loyalty rather than merit.
Post-Retirement Sinecure Culture
•Ministers routinely appointed as Governors, Chairpersons of
Commissions, Ambassadors after leaving office — creating expectation of
reward rather than public service.
•This 'revolving door' undermines independent constitutional bodies like
the National Human Rights Commission, UGC, etc.
Corruption & Misuse of Office
•2G Spectrum, Commonwealth Games, Coal Scam, Fodder Scam, Saradha
Chit Fund
A Ministerial Code for India
• Recommendation: India must enact a Ministerial Code — a published,
enforceable document covering: conflict of interest, use of government
resources, relationship with civil service, standards of conduct.
• Global model: UK Ministerial Code, Canada's Conflict of Interest Act
(2006), Australia's Statement of Ministerial Standards.
• The 2nd ARC (2nd Administrative Reforms Commission, 2005–09)
recommended a comprehensive code of ethics for ministers — yet to be
implemented.
Strengthening Parliamentary Oversight
•Strengthen Departmental Standing Committees: All bills should be
mandatorily referred to Standing Committees (as recommended by M.N.
Venkatachalaiah Commission).
•PMQs-equivalent: Institute a weekly 'PM's Parliament Time' — PM
answers questions in Lok Sabha directly. Enhances accountability.
•Fixed Parliamentary Calendar: Parliament should sit for at least 100
days per year (currently ~65 days). France, Germany, UK all have longer
sitting calendars.
Conflict of Interest Legislation
•Canada: Conflict of Interest Act (2006) — ministers must recuse from
decisions where they have personal interest; assets must be placed in
blind trusts.
•Australia: Ministerial Standards require disclosure of interests; ministers
must divest financial interests in relevant sectors.
•India: No equivalent. NITI Aayog has discussed this; Lokpal does cover
ministers for corruption but not preventive conflict of interest.
Expert Involvement in Governance
•Germany: Ministers are often subject specialists — Finance Ministry
headed by trained economists, Health Ministry by health professionals.
•Singapore: Cabinet draws on elite technocrats; ministers are subject to
performance metrics.
India Reform: Institute a system of Senior Minister-Technical Adviser
pairings — a political minister and a domain expert working together
with shared accountability
Strengthening Individual Ministerial Responsibility
•Enact a 'Ministerial Accountability Act' defining clear triggers for
ministerial resignation — modelled on UK conventions but made
statutory.
•Parliamentary Committees should be empowered to call a vote of
censure on individual ministers — not just the whole government.
New Zealand: Parliamentary Committees have substantial power to
summon and interrogate ministers; model worth studying
Depoliticising Ministerial-Bureaucracy Interface
•Implement Fixed-Tenure for civil servants: Ensure IAS officers cannot
be transferred arbitrarily by ministers — as Supreme Court directed in
T.S.R. Subramanian v. Union of India (2013).
•Civil Services Board: As directed by SC in T.S.R. Subramanian — should
filter political interference in civil service postings.
•UK model: Permanent Secretaries report to both ministers AND
Parliament — dual accountability that prevents capture.
Cabinet Governance Reforms
•Publish a Cabinet Manual: UK Cabinet Manual (published 2011, revised
2022) makes constitutional conventions explicit and enforceable. India
should publish an equivalent.
•Restore Cabinet Committees to formal decision-making: Reduce
reliance on PMO for all decisions — devolve to Cabinet Committees with
published mandates.
•Sunset GoMs: Any Group of Ministers that does not submit a report
within 6 months should be automatically dissolved.
Solutions
The Council of Ministers is not just a constitutional institution — it is the daily exercise of the people's mandate. Every
unaccountable minister, every bloated cabinet, every conflict of interest unaddressed is a democratic debt left unpaid. India's
parliamentary democracy does not need a new constitution. It needs ministers worthy of the one it has.

1. Limited individual dissent : Ministers are expected to publicly support Cabinet decisions, even if they personally
disagree. This restricts their ability to voice individual policy preferences that differ from the collective stance.
a.Example (India): In 2019, Finance Minister Arun Jaitley publicly defended demonetisation, despite some internal
reservations within the government. Under collective responsibility, dissent had to remain private once the Cabinet
decided.
b.Comparative: In the UK, Robin Cook resigned in 2003 over the Iraq War because he disagreed with Cabinet policy —
rather than publicly criticising it while remaining in office.
2.Constrained policy advocacy
a.Example: When Maneka Gandhi (Minister for Women and Child Development) advocated for stricter environmental
norms and animal welfare policies, she had to align them with broader government economic priorities rather than
pursue an independent agenda.
b.Comparative: In the UK, Boris Johnson as Foreign Secretary (2016–18) was often seen as constrained from publicly
advocating a harder Brexit line than the government’s collective position.
3.Pressure for consensus
a.Example: During the 2016 GST rollout, ministers from different portfolios (Finance, Commerce, Consumer Affairs) had
to compromise to present a unified Cabinet stance despite differing economic concerns.
b.Comparative: In the UK coalition government (2010–15), Liberal Democrat ministers frequently compromised to
maintain Cabinet consensus with Conservatives on austerity policies.
4.Resignation as the main recourse
a.Example: Arif Mohammad Khan resigned from Rajiv Gandhi’s Cabinet in 1986 over the Shah Bano case, disagreeing
with the government’s decision to dilute the Supreme Court’s judgment.
b.Comparative: Robin Cook (UK, 2003) and Jim Hacker (fictional “Yes Minister”) both illustrate how resignation is the
ethical route when one cannot abide by collective Cabinet decisions.
5.Emphasis on private persuasion
a.Example: During the 1991 economic reforms, Manmohan Singh privately persuaded sceptical ministers of the
necessity of liberalisation before a collective Cabinet endorsement. Public dissent was avoided.
b.Comparative: In the UK, ministers often use Cabinet committees or informal “sofa government” meetings to influence
decisions behind closed doors.
6.Reduced transparency
a.Example: The Cabinet’s deliberations on Article 370 abrogation (2019) were kept highly confidential; individual
ministers could not publicly discuss their personal reasoning.
b.Comparative: In Australia, Cabinet confidentiality is protected by convention; ministers cannot later reveal internal
disagreements (as seen in debates over COVID-19 response policies).
7.Prime Ministerial influence
a.Example: The dismissal of Shashi Tharoor (2010) following controversy over IPL statements showed how the PM can
enforce collective discipline and limit ministerial autonomy.
b.Comparative: In the UK, Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher’s reshuffles often removed ministers (e.g., Geoffrey Howe)

a.Example: Under Atal Bihari Vajpayee’s coalition government (1999–2004), ministers like Jaswant Singh and Yashwant

b.Comparative: In New Zealand’s Cabinet Manual, the doctrine is explicitly described as fostering “team government,”

9.Accountability trade-offs
a.Example: During the COVID-19 response, criticism of mismanagement of health and migrant issues could not be
pinned on one minister; the entire Cabinet bore collective responsibility.
b.Comparative: In the UK Windrush scandal (2018), responsibility was diffused across departments — demonstrating
how collective accountability can obscure individual responsibility.
10.Policy consistency
a.Example: The Modi government’s uniform policy messaging on welfare schemes (Jan Dhan, Ujjwala, Swachh Bharat)

b.Comparative: In Canada, collective responsibility ensures consistency between federal ministries in bilingual policy
implementation, even if regional ministers face local pressures.

1.Constitutional head of government
a.The PM is the “head of the Council of Ministers,” explicitly mentioned in Article 74.
b.The President is the nominal executive; the PM is the real executive (de facto).
c.All executive action of the Union is taken in the name of the President (Article 77), but the
decision-making is driven by the PM-led Cabinet.
2.Leader of the Lok Sabha majority
a.The PM is usually (not always) a Lok Sabha member and is the acknowledged leader of the majority
there.
b.This gives control over:
i.Confidence of the House
ii.Money Bills / Budget passage
iii.Legislative agenda, timing of bills, guillotine of demands for grants, etc.
3.Chair of the Cabinet : The PM decides:
a.size of the Council of Ministers;
b.distribution of portfolios;
c.Cabinet agenda (what comes up for decision and what never sees the table);
d.core groups / GoMs / EGoMs for inter-ministry coordination;
e.crisis response architecture (war, financial crisis, pandemic).
4.Nodal point between party and state
a.The PM is also usually the most important political figure in the ruling party/alliance.
b.This means they can enforce party discipline in Parliament through the whip system: members vote
as told, or risk disqualification under the Anti-Defection Law (Tenth Schedule).
5.National security, foreign policy, finance core
a.By convention, the PM personally retains some of the highest-stakes portfolios at least in
supervisory terms: PMO is deeply involved in Defence, Home/Internal Security, Finance, External
Affairs, Atomic Energy, Space.
6.First among equals… but only sometimes
a.Classically, Westminster theory calls the PM primus inter pares (“first among equals”).
b.Reality in India has varied by era:
i.High Cabinet government phase: e.g. early Nehru cabinets were debate-heavy with strong
ministers (Patel, Ambedkar, etc.).
c.High PMO centralisation phase: e.g. Indira Gandhi after 1969 split the Congress; decision-making
moved from Cabinet to PMO, creating what scholars call “Prime Ministerial Government.”
d.Coalition-constraint phase: e.g. 1996–2004 (Deve Gowda, Gujral, Vajpayee), PM must constantly
negotiate with allies to survive.
e.Re-centralisation phase in stable-majority governments: A strong-Majority PM tends to centralise
again (coordination through PMO/CMO-style governance, direct monitoring dashboards, etc., to
keep ministries aligned with flagship schemes).
The Prime Minister is the pivot of the parliamentary system, combining political authority with administrative
leadership, and translating the mandate of the people into governance.
Ultimately, the Prime Minister’s role reflects the strength of democratic mandate and maturity of
institutions: the office thrives when it leads collaboratively and governs constitutionally.

130th Constitutional Amendment Bill. 2025
Impacts on ministerial appointments and governance
1.Greater risk for ministers with serious criminal charges
a.Ministers with ongoing prosecution or detention risk
automatic removal after 30 days of custody. That may
deter appointment of those with serious unresolved
charges (depending on political calculations).
b.Parties may screen more carefully before picking ministers
if the Bill becomes law.
2.Incentive for PM/CMs to act quickly
a.The PM/CM will be under pressure: if a minister is
detained, the PM (or CM) must advise removal by 31st day
or face automatic cessation. This reduces discretion.
b.It may lead to more frequent ministerial
resignations/removals tied to legal issues rather than
purely political ones.
3.Potential for political-legal manipulation
a.The trigger is “detention for 30 consecutive days” (not
conviction), there is room for misuse: arrests/detention
may be used for political ends, leading to removal even
before trial/discharge.
b.Critics argue this may shift accountability from legislature
to prosecutorial/detention regime.
4.Impact on appointment strategy
a.PMs may prefer ministers without pending serious
investigations/detentions to avoid risk of destabilisation.
b.May influence coalition negotiations: allies may insist on
clean profile ministries if this law passes.
5.Potential effect on government stability
a.Since a minister’s removal may have broader political
fallout (coalition partners upset, regional representation
imbalance), the Bill adds a new structural risk for the
government.
6.Symbolic effect on integrity narrative
a.The government presents the Bill as enhancing
“constitutional morality” and good governance. For
example, the Home Minister said no minister should “run
the government while being in jail.”
b.This may raise public expectation of ministerial probity,
even if actual effect is limited.
Key critiques, constitutional issues & caveats
1.Presumption of innocence: Critics say removal
based on detention (not conviction) violates the
principle of “innocent until proven guilty.” E.g., one
article:
2.Separation of powers / misuse risk: The fact that
criminal investigation/detention regime can trigger
removal introduces a link between
executive/prosecution machinery and political
office removal. There is risk of arbitrary arrests
leading to forced resignations.
3.Ambiguity in definition of offences: The Bill uses
“offence punishable with 5 years or more” — but
which law? How’s detention defined in each case?
Bail within 30 days appears as a safe-harbour (if
bail granted before 30 days, the trigger may not
apply) but practical variability exists.
4.Automatic cessation vs. political accountability:
The removal mechanism is automatic in absence of
advice; this shifts removal from purely
political/legislative process to a quasi-legal trigger.
That may reduce the role of the legislature
(collective responsibility) in favour of
judicial/legal/detention trigger. This may impact
the doctrine of collective responsibility.
5.Effect on ministerial appointment discretion:
While the Bill is pitched as “raising standards,”
critics fear it may also be used to sideline political
opponents by using arrest/detention as instrument
of removal.
6.Pending status: As of now, it is a Bill — it has not
yet been enacted into law. So the provisions are
prospective
In a democracy, the demand for integrity in public life is non-negotiable. But integrity must be secured through law, not at the cost
of justice. The 130th Amendment is a step toward ethical governance, yet its real test lies not in the text of the amendment, but in
how responsibly the State wields it.

1.Positive impact: Allows Parliament to focus on major

2.Negative impact: Risk of executive overreach; dilution

3.Democratic deficit: If not properly controlled, can

“bureaucratic legislation
Impact on Separation of Powers
1.Traditional view: Separation of powers is blurred as the
executive performs legislative functions.
2.Modern reality: Complete separation is impractical;
delegation is a pragmatic necessity for effective
governance.
3.Constitutional balance: While delegation is permitted,
essential legislative functions (policy-making) must remain
with the legislature. Courts ensure that delegation does
not amount to abdication of legislative responsibility
