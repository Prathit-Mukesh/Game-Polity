Coined by a French Jurist Montesquieu in his book L. Esprit Des Lois (Spirit of Laws) published in 1748
Need for Separation of Power
1.Preventing Tyranny and Abuse of Power: By dividing power among different branches, it becomes much more difficult
for any single person or group to accumulate excessive power and act in an arbitrary or oppressive manner.
2.Protecting Individual Liberty: Separating powers helps safeguard individual rights and liberties by preventing any one
branch from infringing on personal freedoms unchecked. With distinct branches exercising different functions, there
are more barriers to government overreach.
3.Promoting Efficiency and Specialisation: Dividing responsibilities allows each branch to focus on its core functions and
develop specialised expertise. The legislature can concentrate on law-making, the executive on implementation, and the
judiciary on interpretation and dispute resolution.
4.Ensuring Checks and Balances: Each branch from becoming too powerful.
5.Enhancing Accountability: With clearly delineated roles and responsibilities, it becomes easier to hold specific branches
accountable for their actions or failures. This promotes transparency and good governance.
6.Upholding Rule of Law: Especially w.r.t the judiciary, helps ensure laws are applied impartially and consistently. An
independent judiciary can interpret laws without undue influence from the other branches.
"Separation of powers is not a wall — it is a living system of constitutional restraint and dialogue. Without it, the
Constitution is just ink; with it, it becomes justice."

Key Judgments Illustrating the Balance
1. Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) — Parliament's constituent power is coextensive with, not superior to, the people's
will; created Basic Structure Doctrine, checking legislative supremacy while preserving amending flexibility.
2. Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) — Struck down 39th CA (which barred judicial review of PM's election) as violative of free and
fair elections and judicial review — first practical application of Basic Structure; classic example of constitutional morality trumping
political convenience.
3. Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) — Struck down clauses of 42nd CA giving DPSPs unconditional primacy over Fundamental
Rights; held judicial review itself to be part of Basic Structure — a check on Parliament's self-aggrandising amendments.
4. Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992) — Upheld anti-defection law but read down absolute finality of Speaker's decisions,
subjecting them to judicial review — balancing legislative privilege with rule of law.
5. Naz Foundation v. NCT of Delhi (2009) & Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018) — Constitutional morality invoked explicitly
to strike down Section 377, protecting minority (LGBTQ+) rights against prevailing social/popular morality — Judiciary as counter-
majoritarian check.
6. Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala (Sabarimala, 2018) — Constitutional morality (equality, non-discrimination)
held to override religious/popular customary morality; also demonstrated the limits of judicial intervention when social resistance
follows.
7. Joseph Shine v. Union of India (2018) & K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) — Decriminalised adultery and recognised
privacy as a fundamental right, grounding both in constitutional morality of individual autonomy and dignity over legislative/social
morality.
8. SC Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (NJAC Case, 2015) — Struck down 99th CA/NJAC Act as violating judicial
independence (Basic Structure) — but criticised as judiciary protecting its own turf, showing the tension between judicial
independence and democratic accountability.
9. Sita Soren v. Union of India (2024) — Held that legislators do not enjoy immunity under Art 105(2)/194(2) for bribery in
voting/speech — Parliamentary privilege reined in by constitutional morality of clean governance.
10. S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) — Checked misuse of Art 356 (President's Rule), subjecting it to judicial review —
restraining executive/political overreach into federal-democratic structure.
