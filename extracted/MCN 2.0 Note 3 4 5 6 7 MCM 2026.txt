Application and Tests for Basic Structure
Testing Methods: Width Test and Identity Test (M Nagaraj Case)
The Identity Test (Kesavananda):
This is the foundational test — "Does the amendment change the identity of the Constitution so
fundamentally that the document that emerges is not the same Constitution as the one that was in
force before the amendment?" It asks whether the constitutional character has been altered
irreversibly. If yes, the amendment is void.
L. Chandra Kumar, the Court suggested that one must examine the feature’s place in the
constitutional scheme, its object and purpose, and the consequences of denying it.
In M. Nagaraj, the Court distinguished between constitutional axioms and ordinary legal rules. It held
that principles such as secularism and federalism may be constitutional axioms, but service-law
principles like catch-up rule and consequential seniority are not themselves part of constitutional
identity. Therefore, altering those principles did not destroy the basic structure
The Width Test (M. Nagraj v. UoI, 2006): What is the true breadth of the amendment’s impact?
In Nagraj, the SC was examining the constitutional validity of amendments to Art. 16(4A) and 16(4B)
allowing reservation in promotions for SCs/STs. The Court articulated the width test: examining
whether the reach or width of a constitutional amendment transgresses the basic structure.
Specifically, the Court:
Upheld the amendments enabling reservation in promotions
But imposed conditions (quantifiable data on backwardness, inadequacy of representation, and
administrative efficiency) as a BSD-derived limitation on how wide the State's power could be
exercised
Held that these conditions were necessary to ensure the amendment did not destroy the basic
feature of equality under Art. 16
In Jarnail Singh v. Lachhmi Narain Gupta (2018), the SC partially revisited Nagraj, removing the
"backwardness" condition for SCs/STs while retaining inadequacy of representation and efficiency
conditions.
The "Abrogation vs. Abridgment" Test:
Derived from Kesavananda — an amendment may curtail or regulate a constitutional feature
(permissible abridgement) but cannot destroy or abrogate it. The SC looks at degree of damage: if a
feature is merely modified, it survives; if its essence is eliminated, it falls.
The Rights Scrutiny Test (IR Coelho):
If the essence of Articles 14, 19 or 21 is destroyed, the law cannot be saved merely because it is
placed in the Ninth Schedule.
Post-Coelho, when examining laws in the IXth Schedule, a rights-based test operates — whether the
amendment or law taken as a whole damages the essential core of fundamental rights under Arts. 14,
15, 19, 21. This is not just an abstract BSD inquiry but a concrete rights-impact analysis.
Modification v. Damage
Not every amendment affecting a basic feature is invalid. The amendment must damage, destroy,
abrogate or emasculate the basic structure. Reasonable modification is allowed; identity destruction
is not.

Application of Basic Structure Doctrine
Judicial review
Judicial review is one of the clearest basic features. In Minerva Mills, the Court struck down clauses of Article
368 inserted by the 42nd Amendment because they attempted to exclude court review of constitutional
amendments.
In L. Chandra Kumar, the Court held that judicial review by High Courts under Articles 226/227 and by the
Supreme Court under Article 32 is part of the basic structure. Tribunals may act as supplementary institutions,
but they cannot completely replace constitutional courts.
Democracy and elections
In Kihoto Hollohan, the Court recognised democracy, rule of law, and free and fair elections as basic features.
This matters because electoral processes are not merely procedural; they are part of constitutional democracy.
In Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain, the Court used basic structure reasoning to invalidate a constitutional
amendment that sought to immunise the Prime Minister’s election from judicial review. The principle was that
electoral democracy cannot survive if elections to high offices are placed beyond judicial scrutiny.
Ninth Schedule
Waman Rao and Bhim Singhji questioned the use of Article 31B for land ceiling and urban land regulation laws;
the Court accepted land reform goals but insisted that even redistributive legislation could not annihilate basic
structure via blanket immunization.
In Coelho, the Court systematized this by requiring that Ninth Schedule laws be tested against fundamental
rights that themselves form part of basic structure (e.g. equality, judicial review), especially where the impact is
“rights-destroying” rather than merely “rights-limiting.”
Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles
Minerva Mills shows that the Constitution does not treat Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles as
enemies. Fundamental Rights protect liberty and dignity; Directive Principles promote social and economic
justice. Their harmony and balance is part of the basic structure.
Secularism and federalism
While primarily about Presidential Rule under Art. 356, SR Bommai held that secularism and federalism are
basic features. A state government pursuing an anti-secular agenda could be dismissed, but the Court must
scrutinize whether Presidential Proclamation is backed by material. This placed judicial check over executive
action in a domain previously considered non-justiciable.
Independence of judiciary
In cases concerning tribunals (Madras Bar Association) and judicial appointments (NJAC case), judges invoked
basic structure (judicial independence, separation of powers) to strike down or severely limit legislative or
constitutional changes that threatened court autonomy
Striking down amendments
42nd Amendment (clauses of Article 368) in Minerva Mills (amending power and judicial review as basic
structure).
Socialism
The SC in Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra (2024) clarified that Art. 31C's position as
settled by Kesavananda and Minerva Mills remains valid. Further, not all privately owned property constitutes
"material resources of the community" under Art. 39(b), limiting the government's power of acquisition — a
BSD-consistent reading protecting property rights without formally reinstating Art. 31.

Affirmative Impacts
Constitutionalization of Judicial Review Itself: The most important structural consequence of the BSD is that
judicial review is no longer merely a creature of statute or parliamentary grace — it is a basic structural
feature. In L. Chandra Kumar v. UoI (1997), the SC declared Arts. 32 and 226 to be part of the inviolable basic
structure. Parliament cannot, even by constitutional amendment, abrogate the High Courts' writ jurisdiction
or the Supreme Court's constitutional remedies jurisdiction. Judicial review is thus self-perpetuating as a
constitutional institution.
Two-Tier Review: BSD created a two-level judicial review system: (a) ordinary Art. 13-based review of
legislation for FR violations, and (b) BSD-based meta-review of constitutional amendments themselves. This
was a doctrinal leap of historic proportions.
First Application — Indira Gandhi (1975): The first use of BSD to strike down a CA (the 39th CA) established
that even political decisions of the most consequential kind — immunizing the Prime Minister's election from
judicial scrutiny — could be undone through BSD-grounded constitutional review.
Checking Executive Arbitrariness: Through BSD-linked expansion of Art. 21 in Maneka Gandhi v. UoI (1978),
due process guarantees were given constitutional protection — any procedure affecting life and liberty must
be fair, just, and reasonable. This case interlocked Art. 14 (non-arbitrariness), Art. 19, and Art. 21 into a rights
trinity, subjecting all executive action to heightened judicial scrutiny.
Protecting Electoral Integrity: Kihoto Hollohan vs. Zachillhu (1993) upheld the Tenth Schedule on defection
but allowed judicial review, adding free elections and democracy to the basic structure.
Critical or Negative Impacts
Democratic Deficit and the NJAC Controversy: The striking down of the 99th CA in the NJAC Case
represents the most acute friction point between BSD-based judicial review and democratic legitimacy. The
National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) Case (2015): a constitutional amendment that attempted
to alter the judicial appointments system was struck down by the Supreme Court. A constitutional
amendment passed by near-unanimity in both houses of Parliament and ratified by state legislatures was
invalidated by an unelected court — the counter-majoritarian difficulty in its starkest form.
Vagueness and Uncertainty: The Basic Structure Doctrine's vagueness — the undefined scope of "basic
features" — allows judicial discretion but risks inconsistency. New features are added over time through
judicial pronouncement, creating perpetual uncertainty about the outer limits of amending power.
Judicial Encroachment and Overreach: The creation of Vishaka Guidelines (1997) on workplace sexual
harassment, while beneficial in outcome, exemplifies how BSD-related assertions of power have led courts
into legislative territory. The study interrogates the tension between judicial activism and parliamentary
supremacy, questioning whether excessive judicial reliance on constitutional morality risks creating a
counter-majoritarian bias that undermines democratic will.
Policy Disruption: Judicial interventions invoking BSD-adjacent principles have stalled infrastructure projects
(environmental clearances), agricultural reform (farm laws stay in 2021), and government reorganizations —
illustrating that the doctrine's protective power can also become an obstructive force
Impact of Basic Structure on Judicial Review
Normatively, basic structure transforms judicial review from mere legality control to a form of “constitutional
guardianship,” with all the attendant tensions between democracy and juristocracy.

BSD as Democracy's Bulwark
Defence Against Authoritarianism — The Emergency Test: This judgment (Kesavananda) is often given
credit with keeping India a democracy during the difficult years of the Emergency in the 1970s. The
42nd CA's attempt to make Parliament's power unlimited and immunize amendments from review was
precisely the kind of constitutional self-entrenching maneuver that authoritarians use to legitimize
permanent rule. Minerva Mills's response — striking down Arts. 368(4) and (5) — was the constitutional
immune response that kept India's democratic foundations intact.
Free and Fair Elections as Structural Guarantee: The Indira Gandhi (1975) judgment's declaration that
free and fair elections are a basic feature means that no parliamentary majority can insulate the electoral
process from judicial oversight. Subsequent developments — including ADR v. UoI (2002) (disclosure of
criminal/financial backgrounds of candidates), Lily Thomas (2013) (disqualification of convicted
legislators), and Electoral Bonds Case (ADR v. UoI, 2024) — built on this foundation to strengthen
electoral democracy.
Secularism and Pluralism: S.R. Bommai vs. Union of India (1994) declared Federalism, Secularism, and
Democracy as the Constitution's basic structure. By making secularism a basic feature, the SC created a
constitutional check on sectarian governance — a protection for India's plural democracy that ordinary
legislative majorities cannot dismember.
Federalism as Democratic Decentralization: The BSD's protection of federalism as a basic feature
prevents the Centre from using its amending power to convert India into a unitary state, preserving the
democratic multiplicity of state-level governance as a constitutional value.
Multi-Party Democracy Protected: Kuldip Nayar v. UoI (2006) recognized parliamentary democracy and
the multi-party system as basic features — protecting political pluralism from majoritarian constitutional
engineering that might entrench one-party dominance.
BSD's Democratic Challenges
The Counter-Majoritarian Problem: By empowering an unelected judiciary to strike down amendments
passed by elected majorities, the BSD institutionalizes what legal scholars call counter-majoritarianism.
The tension between judicial activism and parliamentary supremacy questions whether excessive judicial
reliance on constitutional morality and BSD risks creating a counter-majoritarian bias that undermines
democratic will.
Constitutional Freezing: By placing certain constitutional features beyond even amendment, the BSD
can freeze power structures that democratic majorities might legitimately wish to reform. The NJAC
episode is the clearest example — the collegium system, itself a judicially created mechanism, was
rendered effectively unamendable through BSD reasoning.
Ambiguity as a Power Tool: The doctrine's open-ended character — the SC continues to identify new
basic features over time — gives the judiciary an expanding portfolio of constitutional veto points over
parliamentary action.
The “abstraction problem” – determining the level of generality at which a feature counts as “basic” –
gives judges broad discretion, increasing the risk that personal or ideological views may be
constitutionalized.
Comparative Perspective: India's BSD is more robust than most comparable constitutional systems.
Germany's "eternity clauses" (Art. 79(3) of the Grundgesetz) are textually specified. South Africa's
constitutional amendment provisions are tiered. India's BSD is entirely judge-made — giving it unusual
flexibility but also exposing it to charges of judicial self-empowerment.
Basic structure can be defended as a form of “militant constitutionalism” necessary in a context of weak party constraints and potential
majoritarian excesses, but it demands judicial self-restraint and clear reasoning to remain democratically defensible

Analysis of the First Constitutional Amendment, 1951
The First Amendment (1951) is a classic case of Parliament attempting to recalibrate rights to facilitate socio-economic
reform, and the later basic structure doctrine retroactively disciplining that attempt.
3 Judicial Decisions triggered : Champakam Dorairajan (1951), Romesh Thappar (1950), Motilal (1951)
Key elements:
Article 19 changes: It narrowed the scope of freedom of speech and expression by adding further “reasonable
restrictions” grounds (e.g. public order, friendly relations with foreign states) and reconfiguring Article 19(2), largely in
response to judgments like Romesh Thappar and Brij Bhushan that had robustly protected press freedom.
Articles 31A and 31B + Ninth Schedule:
Article 31A protected laws relating to acquisition of estates, extinguishment/modification of rights in such estates,
etc., from being void on the ground of contravention of Articles 14, 19 or 31.
Article 31B and the Ninth Schedule validated 13 specific state land-reform laws, retrospectively insulating them from
fundamental-rights challenges; even statutes already held unconstitutional would be deemed valid once listed in the
Schedule.
Democratic legitimacy issues: The amendment was passed by the Provisional Parliament elected on a limited franchise,
raising questions in contemporary scholarship about the legitimacy of such a far-reaching change in rights and property
regimes.
Through the lens of basic structure (though developed later), the First Amendment embodies a foundational tension: using formal
amendment to entrench a transformative socio-economic agenda while also carving out immunity from judicial review. Coelho and
later scholarship argue that this “insulation strategy” ultimately had to be tamed to preserve constitutional supremacy and judicial
review.
Analysis of the IXth Schedule :Constitutional Architecture of Immunity
Ninth Schedule is not a constitutional black hole. It is protected only so long as it does not become a device to destroy constitutional identity.
The IXth Schedule: A Constitutional Safe Harbour : Article 31B, inserted by the First Constitutional Amendment, 1951,
created the Ninth Schedule — a list of laws that were explicitly placed beyond the reach of judicial review on the
grounds of violation of Fundamental Rights. This was a legislative device to immunize land reform laws (and later, other
welfare legislation) from challenge under Articles 14, 19, and 31.
The Ninth Schedule was originally a social revolution device—it protected land reform laws from being struck down by
courts. But over time it became a constitutional immunity device, with Parliament placing diverse laws into it to shield
them from Fundamental Rights review.
The Supreme Court corrected this through Waman Rao and I.R. Coelho. Waman Rao created the 24 April 1973 cut-off
date, the date of Kesavananda Bharati. Laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule before that date received protection; laws
inserted after that date could be tested against the basic structure.

Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) — Defeating Parliament's Counter-Revolution
Background and Context: The 42nd Constitutional Amendment (1976), enacted during the Emergency under PM Indira Gandhi, was a
sweeping attempt to reverse Kesavananda. It amended 54 provisions of the Constitution — a "mini Constitution" — including:
Art. 31C: Expanded from protecting laws implementing Art. 39(b)(c) to protecting all DPSPs from Arts. 14, 19, and 31
Art. 368(4): CAs under Art. 368 could not be challenged in court
Art. 368(5): No limitation on Parliament's constituent power to amend the Constitution
Amendments to Arts. 32, 131, and 226 limiting judicial review (later undone by 43rd CA, 1977)
The SC in Minerva Mills Ltd. v. UoI (1980), Article 368 (5) transgresses the limitation on the amending power of Parliament and is
hence unconstitutional.
Specific Holdings:
Court invalidated parts of the 42nd Amendment, affirming judicial review as integral to the Constitution's basic structure. Arts. 368(4)
and 368(5) were struck down — a Parliament that could immunize its own amendments from judicial review and declare its power
unlimited would destroy the constitutional balance that makes limited amending power itself a basic feature.
Art. 31C was restored to its pre-42nd CA position — only laws implementing Arts. 39(b)(c) (not all DPSPs) could override Arts. 14 and
19, and such laws remained subject to judicial review.
Court established the critical principle that Fundamental Rights and DPSPs must be read harmoniously — neither Part III nor Part IV
can be given such overriding primacy as to annihilate the other. The balance between them is itself a basic structural feature.
The majority's reasoning upheld the Constitution's integrity by protecting the balance between rights and Directive Principles and
reaffirmed the judiciary's role as a check on legislative excess, especially in the post-Emergency context. Justice Bhagwati's dissent
highlighted the socio-economic importance of Directive Principles, arguing that the majority's view might hinder welfare reforms.
Analytical Significance: Minerva Mills represents the most important application of BSD to date, as it neutralised a deliberate
parliamentary attempt to make the basic structure doctrine unenforceable. The principle that limited amending power is itself a basic
feature — and that Parliament cannot expand its own constituent power to infinity — is the bedrock of Indian constitutionalism.
Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981) — The Temporal Bright Line
Background: After Minerva Mills, a critical question remained: what was the status of the hundreds of laws that had been added to the
Ninth Schedule before Kesavananda (1973)? Could they now be challenged? Waman Rao arose in this context, particularly concerning
Kerala land reform laws added by the 29th CA (1971).
Holdings: According to Waman Rao v. UoI, amendments to the Constitution made on or after April 24, 1973, by which the Ninth Schedule
was amended from time to time by inclusion of various Acts and regulations, were open to challenge on the ground that they, or any one
or more of them, are beyond the constituent power of Parliament since they damage the basic or essential features of the Constitution or
its basic structure.
By contrast, laws added to the Ninth Schedule before April 24, 1973 — the date of the Kesavananda judgment — could not be challenged
on BSD grounds, applying the doctrine of prospective overruling to preserve legal certainty in settled land reform transactions.
Analytical Significance: Waman Rao struck a pragmatic balance between two competing constitutional values — the retroactive chaos
that would result from invalidating hundreds of pre-1973 land reforms, and the constitutional supremacy principle that no legislature can
permanently immunize laws from BSD scrutiny. The date of April 24, 1973 became the constitutional watershed line.
I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007) — Final Resolution
Background: The landmark judgment in I.R. Coelho (Dead) by Lrs. v. State of Tamil Nadu, addresses the pivotal question of the
constitutional validity of Article 31-B in the context of the basic structure doctrine. This case revisits the enduring debate surrounding the
Ninth Schedule, specifically scrutinizing whether laws placed under this Schedule can infringe upon fundamental rights without judicial
obstruction.
A 9-judge Constitution Bench was constituted to resolve the tensions in Waman Rao and to definitively answer whether post-
Kesavananda Ninth Schedule laws could be challenged only on BSD grounds or also on specific FRs.
Holdings:
Waman Rao's temporal line (April 24, 1973) was confirmed and upheld.
Laws added to the Ninth Schedule post-April 24, 1973, are not only open to BSD challenge but can also be challenged on violations
of Arts. 14, 15, 19, and 21 specifically — the core guarantees of equality, non-discrimination, freedoms, and life.
There is only fictional immunity in the Ninth Schedule as the law must conform to the basic structure of the Constitution after April
24, 1973. Art. 31B cannot operate as an impenetrable shield for post-Kesavananda laws.
Analytical Significance: The Supreme Court's reasoning pivots around the inherent limitations of Parliament's amending power as
outlined by the Kesavananda Bharati judgment. While Article 31-B historically provided a means to validate certain laws, its broad
language raised concerns about infringing upon the Constitution's basic structure. IR Coelho also reaffirmed Rule of Law and Separation
of Powers as basic features, both cited in the constituent cases table.
The Role of Minerva Mills, Waman Rao, and IR Coelho
These three cases constitute the post-Kesavananda trinity — they operationalized, refined, and finally settled the BSD's scope in
relation to parliamentary overreach and the Ninth Schedule.
Together, the Minerva-Waman-Coelho trilogy represents the jurisprudential maturation of the BSD: from its creation (Kesavananda), through
its defense against parliamentary reversal (Minerva Mills), to its disciplined temporal calibration (Waman Rao), to its final comprehensive form
applicable to the Ninth Schedule (IR Coelho).

Basic Structure Doctrine and Ordinary Legislation
The Court has oscillated between denying and tacitly accepting direct application of basic structure to ordinary laws
The Classic Position: BSD as a Limit on Art. 368
The original BSD framework in Kesavananda was directed exclusively at the amending power.
Extension to Ordinary Laws: Madras Bar Association & Beyond
In Madras Bar Association v. UoI (2015), the SC significantly extended the doctrine's reach by holding that not just
constitutional amendments under Art. 368, but also ordinary laws, cannot violate the Basic Structure. This was a doctrinal
leap — it meant even regular parliamentary legislation could be invalidated if it damaged a basic structural feature.
However, this extension was qualified and disciplined by the SC in Anjum Qadri v. UoI (2024) (UP Madrassa Case), where
the Court held that to invoke the Basic Structure against an ordinary law, one must specifically demonstrate how that
ordinary law violates the basic structure — the test is rigorous and cannot be invoked in vague or generalised terms.
Analysis
Relationship between BSD and ordinary legislation creates a three-tier constitutional hierarchy:
Constitutional Text — Parliament can amend subject to BSD
Basic Structure — constrains both constituent and ordinary legislative power
Ordinary Laws — must conform to Part III (Art. 13), and beyond Part III violations, must also not damage basic structural
features
This means the BSD functions simultaneously as:
A limitation on Parliament's amending power (Kesavananda, Minerva Mills)
A standard of validity for ordinary legislation (Madras Bar Association, Anjum Qadri)
Constitutional Morality, Basic Structure, and Constitutional Amendments
These three concepts are not parallel tracks but interlocking dimensions of a single constitutional idea — that India's Constitution embodies a
moral vision that cannot be destroyed, even by majorities. Basic Structure is the institutionalisation of Constitutional Morality.
Constitutional Morality as the Ethical Foundation of BSD: The Basic Structure Doctrine is the juridical operationalization of constitutional
morality. When the SC in Kesavananda identified certain features as "basic," it was identifying the moral core of the Constitution — features
whose elimination would betray the constitutional promise to the people. BSD provides the doctrinal mechanism; constitutional morality
provides the normative content that informs which features qualify as "basic."
Constitutional Amendments as Moral Acts: Amendments are not merely technical legislative exercises — they are solemn moral choices
about the kind of polity India wishes to be. When such amendments conflict with the deeper moral architecture of the Constitution (as the
42nd CA's Art. 368(4) and (5) did, or the 39th CA's immunity of the PM's election did), BSD — grounded in constitutional morality — corrects
them.
The Preamble as Moral Source: The Preamble's transformation — from not being part of the Constitution (Berubari Union, 1960) to being
its integral source (Kesavananda, 1973) — mirrors the rise of constitutional morality. Most BSD components (sovereignty, secularism,
democracy, justice, liberty, equality) were first sourced from the Preamble's moral vision. This means the Preamble can textually be
amended, but the objectives it expresses — which have acquired BSD status — cannot be destroyed through amendment.
Transformative Constitutionalism as the Bridge: The judiciary has traditionally been at the forefront of social change through the
development of the "basic structure" doctrine and public interest litigation. However, the current situation shows a growing tension
between the judiciary's initial mandate to support a "social revolution" and growing criticism of judicial overreach into the legislative and
executive branches
Its ultimate value lies in the assurance it gives every citizen that the Constitution's promise of justice, liberty, equality, and fraternity — written into
the Preamble with profound deliberation — cannot be erased by any arithmetic of political majority, however large.
