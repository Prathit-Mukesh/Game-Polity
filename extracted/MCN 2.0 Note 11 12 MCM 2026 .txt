Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023
In a democracy like India—diverse, vocal, and increasingly digitized—the right to privacy is not merely a legal shield but a
philosophical cornerstone. It redefines how citizens engage with technology, government, and each other, demanding a new
social contract grounded in trust, transparency, and respect for individuality.
The Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023 is India's first standalone, cross-sectoral law on personal data, enacted
after the Supreme Court recognised privacy as a fundamental right under Article 21 in K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India
(2017) and following the recommendations of the Justice B.N. Srikrishna Committee (2018). The Act was operationalised
through the DPDP Rules, 2025, notified by MeitY
● Applicability: Covers digital personal data processed within India, and processing outside India if linked to offering
goods/services to persons in India (extraterritorial reach, akin to GDPR).
● Consent-centric design: Consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, backed by clear
affirmative action; Deemed Consent permitted for state functions, employment, medical emergencies and legitimate uses.
Notices must use simple, specific language itemising data and purpose.
● Key actors: Data Principals (rights to access, correction, erasure, grievance redress, nominate a representative on
death/incapacity); Data Fiduciaries (tiered obligations); Data Processors (contractually bound); Consent Managers (registered
intermediaries enabling unified consent control); Data Protection Board of India (a fully digital, four-member adjudicatory
body; appeals lie to TDSAT).
● Significant Data Fiduciaries (SDFs): Government-notified entities (by data volume/sensitivity/risk) face enhanced duties —
annual Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) and independent audit, a Data Protection Officer, algorithmic due-
diligence, and possible data-localisation restrictions for specified categories of data.
● Breach reporting: Tiered timeline — initial intimation to the Board and affected principals “without delay,” followed by a
detailed report within 72 hours, covering cause, impact, mitigation and remedial steps.
● Children & persons with disabilities: Verifiable parental/guardian consent required; certain exemptions allow platforms to
process children's data for safety, education and limited safeguards (e.g., ed-tech, healthcare) without full restriction.
● Cross-border transfer: Liberalised, “negative-list” approach — data may flow abroad by default, subject to government-
specified restrictions on transfers to particular countries/entities (a “no-fly list” rather than a whitelist), and continuing
sectoral localisation (e.g., payments data).
● Penalties: Steep, schedule-based fines up to ₹250 crore (failure of security safeguards), ₹200 crore (breach-notification
failure; child-data violations), and ₹150 crore (SDF non-compliance) — among the highest globally relative to GDPR's
turnover-linked penalties.

last revised in 2023. The Union Health Ministry issued only
draft guidelines in 2024 (non-binding).

mentally competent adult specifying refusal of treatment if
they later lose decision-making capacity; judicially recognised,

Practical & Procedural
• Multi-layered process (dual boards, family consensus, magistrate intimation) remains slow outside metro hospitals; relief in
Harish Rana came only via an extraordinary Art. 32 petition — not realistically available to an average family.
• Acute shortage of doctors with requisite experience for medical boards; very few states (e.g. Maharashtra, Goa, Karnataka)
have even constituted the required panel of nominated doctors.
• Families are often forced to take such decisions informally, outside the legal framework, exposing doctors to legal risk and
denying patients a dignified, lawful process.
Ethical & Social
• Risk of coercion: expensive long-term care and weak palliative infrastructure could pressure the elderly, disabled, or
financially dependent toward withdrawal — “disguised abandonment” rather than autonomous choice.
• Best-interests test for incompetent patients is under-specified — no clear evidentiary hierarchy; family wishes assumed
convergent with the patient’s inferred will, without independent assessment.
• India’s fragmented, under-resourced healthcare system and deep family involvement make wholesale adoption of Western
active-euthanasia models socially premature.
Institutional & Legal
• Continuing legislative vacuum: courts have repeatedly urged Parliament to legislate, but India still has no statute on passive
euthanasia or end-of-life care — judicial guidelines remain an interim substitute.
• State-level ombudsman/oversight mechanisms have a mixed record — delays, weak enforcement, public unfamiliarity blunt
effectiveness.
Issue Analysis
Mains Concise Note (MCN) 12 : Life & Liberty
• Comprehensive legislation: Parliament should enact a dedicated End-of-Life Care Act giving statutory force to the
Common Cause / Harish Rana framework, rather than leaving it to repeated judicial revision.
• Digital living-will registry: Register Advance Medical Directives on a national digital portal linked to Aadhaar, allowing
patients to create, update, or revoke directives online with physician validation.
• Hospital-based ethics review: Empower in-house Hospital Ethics Committees (senior doctors + palliative specialist +
neutral third party) to authorise withdrawal within a fixed timeline (e.g. 48 hours), replacing the cumbersome external-board
chain.
• Decentralised, tech-enabled oversight: Replace unproven State-ombudsman models with digital dashboards and
independent medical auditors holding statutory backing.
• Mandatory safeguards: Retain a short cooling-off period and mandatory counselling/palliative-care review to guard against
coercion of vulnerable groups, without undue procedural burden.
• Capacity building & awareness: Integrate end-of-life ethics/law into medical curricula, expand palliative care, and run
public-trust campaigns on advance-care planning — laws fail without awareness.
Reforms / The Way Forward
India need not import an active-euthanasia model to honour its constitutional promise of dignity in life and dying. Refining the existing passive-euthanasia
protocol — statutory backing, digital tools, hospital-based ethics review, and stronger palliative care — can make end-of-life decisions accessible and humane,
consistent with India’s context, while guarding against misuse.
